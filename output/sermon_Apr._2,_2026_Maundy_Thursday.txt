In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. There are two hymns in the church's repertoire that begin with the words, "Pange lingua gloriosi," sing, my tongue, of the glorious something. Most of us only know one of them, the one we will sing tonight at the end of the liturgy as we carry the blessed sacrament to the altar of Repose. "Now my tongue the mystery telling of the glorious body sing." The hymn was written by St. Thomas Aquinas around the year 1264 for the feast of Corpus Christi, which happens to be our feast of title here at Atonement.

But Aquinas did not invent these words. He borrowed them from a hymn written nearly 700 years earlier by a poet named Venantius Fortunatus, the Bishop of Poitiers. In the year 570, the Byzantine Emperor sent a relic of the True Cross to Queen Radegund, who had founded a monastery in Poitiers after fleeing her violent husband, the Frankish King. Fortunatus wrote a hymn for the procession that welcomed the relic, "Sing, My Tongue, the Glorious Battle." It is a hymn about the cross, about wood and nails and blood, about the tree of death that became the tree of life.

Seven centuries later, Aquinas takes those same opening words and turns them in a new direction. Where Fortunatus wrote of the battle, Aquinas writes of the body. Where Fortunatus sang of the cross, Aquinas sings of the altar. The sacrifice on Calvary becomes the sacrament of the Eucharist. The old hymn is not discarded; it is fulfilled. And that is precisely what happens in our gospel reading tonight. Jesus tells his disciples, "I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer." The Greek word translated as "desire" is not a polite expression of mere preference. It is a burning, almost desperate longing. Jesus really wants this meal, and he wants to eat it with these specific people. He wants this night.

Then he shocks his disciples by saying, "I will not eat it until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God." He takes a cup of wine, one of the cups shared during the Passover meal, gives thanks, and says, "I will not drink of the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes." Jesus is saying goodbye to an old meal. He is keeping the Passover one final time, honoring it with love, and then he is letting it go. Not by destroying it, but by fulfilling it on the cross. The Passover was always pointing towards something else. Every element of that ancient meal—the lamb, the bitter herbs, the unleavened bread—was reaching toward a fulfillment it could not yet name. The blood on the doorposts was always a foreshadowing of a greater deliverance.

Tonight, in the upper room, the sign gives way to the reality. Not because the sign was empty; the Passover was and remains one of the most profound acts of worship in human history, but because the one to whom it pointed has finally arrived. "This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me." The ancient meal yields to the new covenant. The Passover lamb is fulfilled in the Lamb of God. Note that Luke uses the word "new" twice in quick succession: a new commandment, a new covenant. Everything old is being made new. Not replaced, not repudiated, but brought to its intended fulfillment.

There is a line in the penultimate stanza of the Aquinas hymn we will sing tonight that captures this, roughly translated as, "types and shadows have their ending, for the newer right is here." The Latin verb "chedat," translated as "ending," is carefully chosen. Ending, though, does not mean to be abolished or destroyed; it means to make room for, to give way. The old covenant cedes to the new, not in defeat, but in glorious fulfillment. The Passover steps aside not because it failed, but because it succeeded. It did what it was meant to do. It pointed to the Messiah. And now that the Messiah is here, the sign gives way to the reality it signifies.

This pattern, the old yielding to the new, is woven into the fabric of tonight's liturgy. We began this evening with the Gloria, the great hymn of praise that has been suppressed throughout Lent. Tonight it returned, one final time before the long silence of Good Friday and Holy Saturday. We will not hear it again until I intone it at the Easter Vigil on Saturday night. The bells rang during the Gloria tonight and then fall silent, replaced by the stark wooden clacker called a crotalus until Easter. Joy gives way to sorrow. Light gives way to darkness.

At the end of tonight's liturgy, we will strip the altar. Every book, every cloth, every candle will be removed while the choir chants Psalm 22: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? They divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." In this most poignant moment, I have the solemn duty of washing the altar as if I were preparing the body of Jesus for his burial. But before the stripping of the altar, there is the procession. The consecrated bread, the body of Christ, will be carried through the church and around to the altar of Repose over here in the chapel. 

As we process, we will sing that hymn Aquinas wrote for such a moment: "Now my tongue, the mystery telling of the glorious body sing, and the blood, all price excelling, which the Gentiles, Lord and King, in a virgin's womb once dwelling, shed for this world's ransoming." Pay close attention to what we are singing as we process. The hymn moves through the entire story of salvation history in six short verses: incarnation, mystery, the Last Supper, real presence, and adoration. It is the gospel compressed into a single hymn. And it builds to the line I keep returning to: "Types and shadows have their ending, for the newer right is here." 

The procession and the hymn are doing the same thing, carrying the old into its fulfillment. Fortunatus's cross becomes Aquinas's altar. The Passover becomes the Eucharist. This pattern of the old yielding to the new did not end in the upper room. It is the very shape of the Christian life. We who gather tonight are ourselves always in need of yielding, letting go of our old complacencies, our old certainties, our old desire to be served rather than to serve, which is perhaps why Luke places what comes next exactly where he does.

The disciples argue about which of them is the greatest. Jesus has just given them his body. He has just told them that one of them will betray him, and their response is to compete for status. Jesus answers them quietly, "The kings of the Gentiles lord it over them, and those in authority over them are called benefactors, but not so with you. The greatest among you must become like the youngest, and the leader like the one who serves, for I am among you as one who serves."

When a priest celebrates the sacraments—baptizing, absolving, consecrating bread and wine—the church says that the priest acts in persona Christi, in the person of Christ. But notice what happens next in tonight's liturgy. Before I stand at the altar and say, "This is my body," I will kneel on the floor and wash many of your feet, just as Jesus washed his friends' feet in that upper room. The basin comes before the altar. The kneeling in humble service comes before the consecrating because Jesus came among us as one who serves.

Tonight, dear friends, everything old is yielding to something new. An ancient promise finds its fulfillment in a new covenant. An old hymn becomes a new song. Old types and shadows are fulfilled in the newer right. An old understanding of power, one that the church is as susceptible to as anyone, becomes a new vision of service. And an old world, groaning under the weight of sin and death, begins tonight in this room at this altar to give way to the kingdom of God. May we, sustained on our journey by his body and blood, follow him into the night and stay awake with him.
