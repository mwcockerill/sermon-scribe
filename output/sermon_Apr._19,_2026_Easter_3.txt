In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Imagine two people walking away from the worst week of their lives. They aren't running, as running would require a destination. They are simply moving, the way that grief makes you move—putting one foot in front of the other because standing still is somehow worse. The road to Emmaus stretches seven miles from Jerusalem, and every step takes them further from the place where it all fell apart. They are sad, confused, and full of despair.

As they are talking and discussing the fateful events they had witnessed, Jesus shows up. But for some reason, their eyes are kept from recognizing him. He asks them what they're talking about, and they respond by telling him about their shattered expectations. Jesus then begins to teach the two travelers and explain to them how the events they had experienced were the fulfillment of the scriptures of old. They still aren't convinced, but when Jesus tries to leave and head another direction, the two disciples urge him strongly to stay with them. Even though they still don't recognize him, his preaching and teaching from the scriptures apparently had enough impact on them that they want to spend more time with him.

When they sit down at the table, he breaks bread, blesses it, and gives it to them. And suddenly their eyes are opened. They realize that the man standing before them is the one in whom they had placed all their hope. The women who experienced the vision with the angel weren't insane after all. He has risen indeed. Jesus finally makes himself known to them in the breaking of the bread.

Now don't mishear me. As a priest, my duties include preaching and teaching the scriptures, as I'm doing now, and that certainly has its place. But preaching and teaching only reach so far into the depths of our being. From the age of 11 to 24, I was Southern Baptist and was taught that the normal way to commune intimately with God is by having what is called a quiet time—time by yourself in a room with the Bible, praying and studying the scriptures. Of course, corporate worship was important, and listening to sermons far lengthier than the one you are enduring now. But for me, anyway, there was something missing that I couldn't quite put my finger on until the day that I walked into a 13th-century church in Paris called Saint-Etienne-du-Mont, Saint Stephen of the Mount.

I was living in Paris and serving as a Baptist missionary, and I sang in a university choir. My first concert with them was in that very church, the one served for many years by the composer Maurice Duruflé and his wife, Marie Madeleine, as organists. During that concert, it dawned on me in an intensely emotional way that we were singing a choral mass setting in a building where the mass had been celebrated every day for nearly 500 years. I had seen the mass times on the way in and decided to attend their simple daily mass the next morning at 8:30 a.m. It was at that service that I first recognized the Lord in the breaking of the bread.

When the priest elevated the consecrated host for all to behold and said, "Behold the Lamb of God, behold him who takes away the sins of the world," my eyes were opened, and I knew what it was that I had been missing. Part of what hit me that day is that salvation is not merely a spiritual redemption of our hearts; it is also a physical redemption of our bodies. God's grace is more than a feeling. And thank God, because sometimes I wondered whether the funny feeling right here is the voice of God or indigestion. God sent Jesus to redeem our whole selves—heart, soul, mind, and body. He doesn't only share his love and grace with us by intangible means, like quiet prayer and the study of scripture, but perhaps even more importantly, by the gritty, tangible elements of water, bread, and wine.

God didn't send his Son to redeem us as a disembodied spirit, but as a human being with flesh and blood and bones. He doesn't leave us wondering whether the voice we are hearing is the voice of God or a ghost. He has given us the sacraments of the church, especially baptism and Holy Communion, as a sure and certain means by which we receive his inward and spiritual grace. As soon as their eyes were opened, the two disciples got up that same hour and went to find their friends, joyfully telling them that the Lord has risen indeed and that he had been made known to them in the breaking of the bread.

This sharing of the faith, sometimes called evangelism, conjures up images of street preachers with megaphones and door-to-door missionaries in foreign countries. But evangelism at its best is the natural sharing of the faith with those you already know and have a relationship with. While it should be intentional, evangelism should never be judgy or pushy or disrespectful. When we encounter God in our lives—whether it's through prayer, through the embrace of a friend, through the scriptures, or through the consecrated bread and wine of communion—it is natural to want to share the joy of that encounter with others, both in word and deed.

Here at Atonement, we celebrate the Eucharist every day, and the Eucharist is not the possession of a few, but the inheritance of all the baptized. Week after week, day after day, Jesus makes himself known to us in the breaking of the bread. I celebrate the Mass usually four or five times a week, and it is quite common for me to elevate the consecrated host and be taken back to that morning in Paris when I was 23 years old, when I first recognized Jesus in the breaking of the bread.

No matter how isolated or lonely or full of despair you may feel, no matter how your hopes and expectations may have been crushed, like the two travelers on the road to Emmaus, you are invited to recognize Jesus here at this altar. We break bread together not in isolation but in community with one another. God meets you where you are, no matter where you are in your journey, no matter your station in life. As we break bread together in a moment, let us ask the Lord to open the eyes of our faith. You may have heard the scriptures preached and taught throughout your life and yet find that something is still missing. You may recognize Jesus today in the breaking of the bread as you have for decades. Wherever you find yourself today, he is here, and he is making himself known to us in the breaking of the bread.
