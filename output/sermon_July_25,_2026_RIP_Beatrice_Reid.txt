Martha's brother had been dead for four days. Jesus could have come. He heard that Lazarus, his friend, was ill and stayed where he was for two more days. By the time he arrives on the road outside Bethany, it is too late, and everyone there knows it. Martha hears he is coming, pulls herself together in the midst of her grief, and walks out to him. The first thing she says is, "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." 

Jesus tells her that her brother will rise again. Her response is clear: "I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day." Martha is a well-taught woman; she is good in her theology. She can produce it on demand, standing in the road four days into the worst week of her life. Yet, it is not helping her in her grief. Jesus doesn't correct her. He doesn't say, "You have it wrong." What she said was true. Her problem is that it is a thing that happens rather than someone who is here. It is still an event she is waiting for, and her brother is dead now. The distance between those two is exactly where she is standing and grieving. 

It is where we are standing this afternoon as we grieve the loss of Aunt B. Jesus doesn't respond by correcting her or fixing her theology; he gives her himself. "I am the resurrection and the life." Martha spoke of an event, a thing that will happen later to people at a certain time. Jesus answers with a person: "I am the resurrection and the life, me, the one standing right in front of you." Saint Augustine sums it up in five words: "The resurrection because the life." Life comes first; resurrection follows it because of who Jesus is. 

Take him out of the equation, and there is no resurrection to have. Leave him in it, and the resurrection is not a date on a calendar but a man on a road coming to find a grieving woman four days too late. Then he asks her, "Do you believe this?" The light bulb goes off in her head and her heart. She answers his question not with a yes or no but with a person: "I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God." 

Here’s what that means for you and me today: we do not have to have all of this figured out to be held by God in the midst of our grief. Some of you are here this afternoon with a deep, well-formed faith. Some have questions you've never said out loud. Some of you came because you love Beatrice, and you aren't of any particular faith. I want you to know that you are most welcome, and I'm glad you're here. Some of you are in a place where you cannot think straight at all, where grief has made a fog of everything. If someone were to stop you at the door and ask you what you believe will happen at the end of time, you would not have been able to tell them. 

Well, Martha could have. She answered correctly on the spot in one of the worst weeks of her life. Yet her correct answer is not what carried her. What carried her was that person she was talking to, that he was the resurrection and the life—not her understanding of him, but him. This is what the gospel offers us today, and it is no small thing. Your grip on God is not what saves you; God's grip on you is what saves you. 

In a few moments, we will sing that familiar hymn, "Amazing Grace." "Through many dangers, toils and snares, I have already come. 'Tis grace has brought me safe thus far, and grace will lead me home." Safe thus far not because we have it all together, but because God's grace has hold of us, and God's grace does not let us go at the graveside. Grace that will lead us home. 

Beatrice was the last of eleven siblings. Ten of them went ahead of her, and she was the last one to remember them. She spent her whole life going back—back to Greenwood, back to family reunions, back to the people she came from. She has made that trip one last time. She did not get there on the strength of what she understood; she got there because the one who is the resurrection and the life came looking for her and found her. 

In the midst of our grief, Jesus says to us, as he did to Martha, "I am the resurrection and the life. Those who believe in me, even though they die, will live. And everyone who lives and believes in me will never die. Do you believe this? Do you believe this?" 

In a few moments, the one who asks that question comes to this altar and gives himself to us, to give us grace in the midst of our grief and a foretaste of the heavenly banquet that Aunt Bea is enjoying in heaven.
