In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. About two weeks ago, the Bishop of Rome published an encyclical called *Magnifica Humanitas*, or *Magnificent Humanity*, on safeguarding the human person in the age of artificial intelligence. In my circles right now, AI is all the rage, with views running the gamut from those who use it constantly to those who would be appalled to hear that I used it to proofread this sermon. Humanity, the Pope writes, faces a pivotal decision: to build a new Tower of Babel or to build the city in which God and humanity dwell together. Babel or Jerusalem?

Last Sunday, Mother Anne gave us exactly the right phrase for our moment. She said, “God the Father or Mother, not God the manufacturer, is our source.” We are God's children, not products made by a celestial machine. We live in a moment when the machines we have built can write our emails, draft our last will and testament, and even diagnose our illnesses. AI can imitate the way human beings think and talk so well that we might be lulled into thinking that the AI model we are talking to is a real person. The deeper question, the one Pope Leo presses, is whether we will let ourselves be remade in AI's image or remember that we are made in the image of God.

The feast we keep today is the Church's answer to the question *Magnifica Humanitas* raises. What is the human person? What does it mean to be made by God rather than assembled by a machine? The answer to that question will appear on the altar in a few moments. But before we get there, a word about why we keep this feast at all. Jesus instituted the Eucharist on the night before he died, on Maundy Thursday. Those of you who were here that night know that the joy of that moment gets crowded out by the shadow of the cross. The grief of the Garden of Gethsemane and the stripping of the altar in the dark leave little room for joy.

So, in the year 1264, after decades of advocacy by a Belgian nun named Juliana of Liège, Pope Urban IV instituted the Feast of Corpus Christi as a more joyful celebration of this amazing gift we have been given. Here are St. Paul's words from our Epistle reading again: “I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took a loaf of bread.” Two of the Greek words in this sentence, translated as “I received” and “I handed on,” are technical verbs, the vocabulary of receiving and delivering a fixed authoritative tradition. Saint Paul is quoting the liturgy of the early church. He is quoting words that were already old when he heard them, almost certainly within a few years of Jesus' crucifixion.

I will speak the same words at the altar in a few moments, words that have come down to us by an unbroken chain of human bodies. Not as data transferred from a server, not as something portrayed on a screen, but from mouth to ear, from hand to hand, from the apostles to their successors, from a bishop in Scotland to a bishop in Connecticut to a bishop in Kansas, to a parish priest in Edgewater. The same words spoken and heard by human beings made in God's image over and over again for nearly 2,000 years. No algorithm or chatbot could do this for us.

Then comes a warning: “Whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be answerable for the body and blood of the Lord. Examine yourselves. For all who eat and drink without discerning the body eat and drink judgment against themselves.” “Unworthy manner” is an adverb. It modifies the manner of our eating, not the worthiness of the person eating. St. Paul is not saying that some of us are worthy to receive communion and some of us are not. None of us is worthy to gather up the crumbs under God's table.

Discerning the body in this passage carries a double meaning. It means recognizing Christ truly present in the consecrated bread and wine against the evidence of your eyes. It also means recognizing Christ in the neighbor kneeling beside you as you receive communion, which is what the Corinthians were failing to do, humiliating the poor at the Lord's Supper. To discern the body is to see Christ both on the altar and in the person next to you. No matter their race, tribe, or gender, every one of them is a person whom no machine can love.

Notice that what discerning the body requires of us in both senses is attention, presence, a body in a place, eyes that see, ears that hear, and a tongue that tastes. None of this is something a machine can do for you. Our current moment with AI certainly isn't the first time we've been faced with such a question. Both moments, in my mind at least, the COVID lockdown and our present AI moment, ask us to accept a simulation of a person as the real thing. When COVID closed our churches, everyone was hungry for the sacraments, and we priests so badly wanted to give them to you.

In some corners of the Episcopal Church, this led clerics to attempt to consecrate the Eucharist virtually over the livestream, with the priest in the church and the bread and wine being held up toward the television or the computer by parishioners sitting at home. We even had one bishop authorize this practice, only to quickly rescind the authorization after having been rebuked by his fellow bishops, including the presiding bishop. However loving and generous the intention was, it simply isn't possible to confect a sacrament from afar. The incarnation of God in the world is inherently embodied, and thus so are the sacraments. They require physical presence.

St. Augustine, preaching in fourth-century North Africa, said it this way: “You see on God's altar bread and a cup. That is what the evidence of your eyes tells you. But your faith requires you to believe that the bread is the body of Christ and the cup, the blood of Christ.” He continues, “If you want to understand the body of Christ, you must listen to the apostle. You are the body of Christ and individually members of it. If that is so, it is the sacrament of yourselves that you receive. You hear the words, ‘the body of Christ,’ and you reply, ‘Amen.’ Be then a member of Christ's body so that your amen may accord with the truth. Be what you see and receive what you are. The sacrament of yourselves.”

The Word became flesh, says Saint John, and dwelt among us. The flesh he took was the flesh of a specific body, in a particular place in history, a body that hungered, tired, bled, and finally died. And the human flesh he became is now our food, and what we eat is what we become. Mother Anne was right: the Father, not the manufacturer, is our source. A manufacturer produces a product and then walks away, while a parent brings a child into the world and knows her, names her, and loves her. To be loved by name and to love in return is something that machines cannot do.

God so values human bodies that he took one himself and gives one back to us here at the altar so that when we receive him into our bodies, we may be transformed more and more into his likeness and sent out to be his body in the world. Dear friends, the Church is not building the Tower of Babel here on earth; we are building the holy city, the heavenly Jerusalem, where God and humanity dwell together. And so I invite you to come to this altar, a place no chatbot can reach, and hear the words spoken by a real person: “The body of Christ,” and answer “Amen” with your own voice.
