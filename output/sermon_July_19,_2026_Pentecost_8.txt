Last week, we heard the parable of the sower, and this week we hear the parable of the weeds, or the tares, as it is traditionally known. Despite having attended a rural Texas high school whose biggest student club was the Future Farmers of America, I am a city boy at heart and don't know the first thing about farming. Thankfully, others have written about some of the farming issues involved in this parable.

It begins with Jesus saying that the kingdom of heaven may be compared to someone who sowed good seed in his field. But while everyone was asleep, an enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat and then went away. The weeds here are thought to be a specific plant called bearded Darnell. This plant looks identical to wheat above ground, but below, it wraps itself around the wheat, intertwining itself into the root system and taking away nutrients and moisture from the wheat. It is a deceptive little plant. Rather than producing seeds that are nutritious like wheat, Darnell seeds are toxic to humans and can cause anything from hallucination to death.

In Jesus' time, sowing Darnell in a field for the purposes of revenge was illegal, meaning it must have happened often enough to warrant such a law. In this parable, Jesus uses this noxious weed to illustrate evil. An enemy intentionally sowed these weeds among the wheat. When the farmhands discovered this, they proposed to the farmer that they go and gather the weeds. Well, like me, these farmhands were not up to speed on their horticulture. If they had been, they would have known that doing so would result in the wheat coming out of the ground at the same time, destroying the crop.

All too often, you and I become preoccupied with who is in and who is out. Like the farmhands, we think that we have what it takes—spiritually, mentally, emotionally—to separate the wheat from the weeds. But as in the parable, trying to separate the wheat from the Darnell actually hurts both plants. This phenomenon happens in the Church today when we try to separate the real Christians from those who have a different stance than we do on any particular issue. In trying to weed out those who are impure, we do damage to the faithful as well.

In the Church, our world is full of dichotomies, and it seems increasingly so, perhaps especially in politics. What is the solution to this tendency to divide the world in two? The farmer instructs the farmhands to let both the wheat and the weeds grow together until the harvest, when all will be revealed. For at the harvest, the wheat and Darnell bloom differently, and it becomes obvious which is which. Even then, it won't be up to us to decide who is in and who is out. Jesus tells us in the second part of the reading that the Son of Man will send his angels to do this work. Then and now, it is our job to trust that God will sort through the wheat and the weeds in a way that only God can do.

No matter how similar they look, no matter how intertwined the root systems are, God will send his angels, with the appropriate knowledge and skill, to separate the weeds out without damaging the wheat. While this parable is explicitly about the kingdom of heaven and thus the last things, I can't help but think that my own life is often like the farmer's infested field, with weeds and wheat intertwined in my heart, soul, and mind. Paul said it this way in the book of Romans: "For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate."

When someone begins to come to private confession regularly for the first time in their lives, it usually only takes two or three confessions before he or she says to me something like this: "Father, I keep confessing the same sins over and over. Is that normal?" Yes, yes it is. All of us are plagued with the temptation to commit specific sins over the course of our lives, each of us influenced by both nature and nurture. And no matter how holy we become, it is unlikely that our respective pet sins will change over time.

Usually, the next question is, "Well, since I keep confessing the same sins over and over, why do I do this at all?" Not to force an analogy into this parable, but perhaps the grace the penitent receives from God through absolution is like the water and nutrients the plants in the field receive for their sustenance. When we repent from our sins and intend to amend our lives, we only do so by God's grace. While we can't stop sinning completely on this side of the veil, we certainly can learn to trust God more and more in the midst of the sin and chaos of our hearts and of the world, holding fast to the hope that at the last day, God will send the reaper to bring in the harvest.

Last week, we heard St. John Chrysostom say that the sower keeps sowing on rocky ground because with human souls, the ground can change. Preaching on today's parable, he goes one step further—frankly further than botany allows. The master spares the weeds, St. Chrysostom says, because it is possible for the weeds themselves to become wheat. We know that bearded Darnell simply cannot become wheat. But grace does what plants cannot do, and the long delay before the harvest is not negligence on God's part; it is the space God has carved out for conversion—yours and mine, and even the person you were most tempted to yank up by the roots this week.

Every season the field grows unweeded is a season grace is still at work beneath the surface, down among the roots too tangled for anyone but God to figure out. That is why the Master waits. One more season, one more confession, one more time to receive Holy Communion. And so we wait too, drinking in the grace of God's Word and sacraments, growing toward a harvest we know will come but cannot yet see.

In a few moments, with wheat and weeds still intertwined in our hearts, we will present ourselves humbly before God at this altar rail, and God will feed us generously without first sorting us out. In the reading we heard from Romans, Paul promises that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory about to be revealed. At the harvest, what has been hidden all season will finally be seen—not the weeds, but the wheat that God, through His extravagant grace, has been making of us all along. Amen.
