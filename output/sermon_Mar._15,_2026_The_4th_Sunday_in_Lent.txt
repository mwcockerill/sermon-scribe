In our first lesson, Samuel is grieving. He has poured years of his life into Saul, anointed him, defended him, and prayed for him. And Saul has failed spectacularly. God has rejected him. Now the Lord comes to Samuel, not with comfort, but with a new commission: "Fill your horn with oil and set out." Mourning what is over, there is a new king to be anointed, and he is waiting in Bethlehem in the household of a man named Jesse.

Jesse proceeds to parade his sons before the prophet, and Samuel, seasoned man of God that he is, takes one look at the eldest, tall and strong, and thinks, "That is the one." Well, the Lord has other things in mind. "Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature," He says, "because I have rejected him. For the Lord does not see as mortals see. They look on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart." Samuel, the one who hears directly from God, the one who anointed the first king of Israel, nearly makes the same mistake twice. He very nearly chooses another Saul. 

Seven sons pass before him, seven—the number of completeness. All seven are rejected. Samuel, apparently not having learned his lesson, asks if there are any more sons. Jesse almost apologetically mentions the youngest. "He's out in the field with the sheep," he says, not even worth summoning for a feast honoring such a prophet. When David finally walks in, the Lord says simply, "Rise and anoint him, for this is the one," the one nobody sent for, the one whose own father didn't think to include. This is the Lord's anointed.

Note the same pattern playing itself out in today's very long gospel reading from the book of John. Jesus walks along and sees a man who has been blind from birth. Nobody brought this man to Jesus. Nobody asked Jesus to heal him. The disciples don't even see a person; they see a theological problem. "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents?" They have walked past a human being and arrived immediately at an abstract question about causality and guilt. Well, Jesus refuses their framework entirely. "Neither this man nor his parents sinned." And then, without fanfare, He stops. He sees and He acts.

This is the heart of both stories, and it is where Lent presses hardest on us. The question is not simply whether we believe that God sees what we cannot. The question is whether we are willing to examine how we see, what we notice, what we pass by, and who we never think to send for. The disciples look at the blind man and see a sinner or the son of a sinner. The Pharisees look at the healed man and see a problem, a disruption to the order they have carefully constructed. Jesse looks at his sons and doesn't even bother to call the youngest in from the field. Every one of them is looking, but none of them is seeing.

I lived in Paris, France, for two years after college. My daily walk took me past medieval churches and Roman ruins, the kind of things that stop American tourists cold in their tracks. Within a few months, I stopped seeing them. When friends came to visit from home, they would stare from the sidewalk with their jaws wide open, pointing at something I had been walking past every day without a second glance. The beauty, of course, hadn't gone anywhere; I had simply learned not to notice it. Familiarity does that, and it does it not only with old stone and glass, but with people.

Lent is the season the Church sets apart for exactly this kind of uncomfortable inventory—the inventory of the dramatic sins; these tend to be obvious enough—but the inventory of the perceptual ones. The ways we have categorized the people around us so efficiently that we no longer actually see them. The people we have written off as unlikely candidates for God's grace, for leadership, for belonging. The ones we didn't think to send for. Who are they in your life? Who are they in ours as a parish? 

Note that this is a very specific question. It is not, "Do I love my neighbor as myself?" in the abstract, but "Whom have I not looked at recently?" The person at work whose name I have never learned? The parishioner on the margins of this community whom nobody quite includes? The family member whose value I have quietly decided is limited? A fellow parishioner here at Atonement whose grief or whose anger I have not thought to take seriously because it doesn't look like mine?

Well, the Pharisees in John 9 are not simply villains; they are competent, learned, devout men. What they cannot do—what their very competence prevents—is see what they do not expect to see. Their categories have become walls, and that is a danger for all of us who think we are learned, devout, and are reasonably sure we know what faithfulness looks like. 

The one consolation in all of this is that the God who sees the heart does not wait for us to see correctly before acting. He sends Samuel to Bethlehem before Samuel has any idea what he is looking for. He walks past the blind man and sees him before any disciple sought to look. The divine gaze precedes and corrects our human gaze. The Lord looks on the heart and, in so doing, calls into question everything we thought we knew about who matters and who doesn't.

This Eucharist that we are about to celebrate is the table set by that same Lord. It is, by definition, the table where the overlooked are most especially welcome, where the youngest son, finally summoned in from the fields, finds that the feast has been waiting for him all along. The Lord does not see as mortals see. They look on the outward appearance, but the Lord looks on the heart.
