In the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, Amen. The other day, I witnessed a beautiful moment in American society. We live right next to the lake, and we often watch what's called "parking wars." These are the conflicts that break out in the Park District parking lot. Usually, these moments are ugly. Someone swoops in to take a spot at the last second while another driver has been waiting patiently for ten minutes. People argue about which direction to head in a narrow lot with no arrows painted on it, and tempers flare when people have had too much sun, too much alcohol, and probably too much family time.

The moment I witnessed started out like many of the ugly ones do. A woman had backed her car onto one of the poles meant to stop drivers from backing into a fire hydrant. Trying to get free, she pressed the gas as hard as she could, and her tires just spun while her car pivoted on the pole. Then she got out, her young child on her hip, looking worried, embarrassed, and a little more than annoyed. For a moment, nobody moved. It would have been easy to just watch, pull out a phone, or drive around and let someone else deal with it—that's usually how it goes. But then a small group started to form. They tried brute strength at first, attempting to lift the car free, but they quickly failed. Then more people gathered, and one person clearly emerged as a leader. Heads came together to figure out how to get the car loose.

Watching this, the woman looked a little less worried, a little less embarrassed, and a little less annoyed. She was able to step back and tend to her child while the crowd worked on the problem. After ten minutes of planning and repositioning, they had enough strategy and strength to lift the car while someone else drove it off the pole. Just as quickly as she had gotten stuck, she was driving off, and the crowd dispersed in every direction. What made this a beautiful moment was that a group of strangers—different races, and since this was Rogers Park, probably different countries of origin, class, and religious backgrounds too—chose each other over the easier option of just walking away. All the things we are told make us too different to get along, and in that moment, none of it mattered. They operated as one.

That's the kind of division Paul was writing into when he wrote to the church in Rome. The emperor had expelled the Jewish population from the city years earlier. While they were gone, the Christian house churches became largely Gentile. By the time Paul writes, the Jewish Christians have returned, and the church is trying to figure out how to be one body again after years of growing apart. It's not so different from a parking lot full of strangers who had no obvious reason to help each other.

Paul opens by asking the Roman Christians to "present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship." He describes worship as something active and lived out, not something that stays in a person's head. The Greek word translated as "spiritual" here can also mean "reasonable" or "logical." His audience would have understood him contrasting the dead animal sacrifices of the temples with the sacrifice of a living body offered to a living God, and hearing the second one made far more logical sense. As Peter rightly declares in today's Gospel, Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of the Living God.

That crowd in the parking lot offered something like a living sacrifice. Nobody was asked to help; they just got out of their cars on a hot day for a stranger because something in them recognized that was what the moment required. That is what it looks like when worship stops being a posture we hold on Sunday and becomes a body we offer the rest of the week. Paul goes on to ask the church to be transformed by the renewing of your minds so that you may discern what is the will of God. He knew the ethnic and cultural differences in that congregation were creating real tension, so he tells them to keep their minds open to discerning God's will together.

Christ points us toward that will when he says the whole law can be summed up in loving God and loving our neighbor. A renewed mind is one that stops treating difference as a reason for distance. That's the harder half of this text for us because it's easier to admire a crowd helping a stranger than it is to let our minds be renewed toward the people in our own pews we've decided we don't have much in common with. Paul isn't asking the Roman Church to pretend their differences don't exist; he's asking them to stop letting those differences decide who is worthy to show up for.

Finally, Paul returns to the image of the body, stating, "For as in one body we have many members, and not all the members have the same function, so we, who are many, are one body in Christ, and individually we are members one of another." Paul isn't erasing distinctions; he's saying they matter. God transforms our minds so that we can act according to the role each of us is suited to play in caring for one another. In that parking lot, one person emerged as a leader, others followed, and others still just held a child. Nobody did everything, but everybody did something. That's the body Paul is describing—sameness, but everyone's particular gifts aimed at the same rescue.

Paul's words to the Romans still hold true today. Our churches remain sites of segregation by race, class, sexuality, country of origin, or ideology. Given how divided we still are, it can be hard to see ourselves as one body. And my friends, I'm not saying that atonement has reached perfection, but I do believe we are a church that tries to be one of real transformation. We open this table to nourishment every day. We work to move past artificial differences in this building and welcome people across every diversity of thought, background, and appearance. Just yesterday, we continued our tradition of feeding our neighbors and offering embodied love and community.

So here's the question I want to leave you with: Who is that person you've already decided isn't part of your body to carry? This week, let that decision be renewed. Show up for them like they're a member of your own body. Show up, offer your hands, and be a living sacrifice to somebody. Because we need more moments of beauty in this world—the kind where a body of strangers becomes one body for as long as it takes to lift what someone else couldn't lift alone. So go and be that for someone.
