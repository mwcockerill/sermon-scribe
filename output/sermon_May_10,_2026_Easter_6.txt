Amen. Take a look at the cover of your bulletin. The image you see there is a mosaic from an Orthodox church in Cyprus. The Greek word for this icon is Anastasis, meaning "the resurrection." This is the standard image of Easter in every Eastern Orthodox parish in the world, and it is not at all what I picture when I think of the resurrection. There is no empty tomb there. There are no angels in white. There is no garden. Christ stands instead on the broken doors of hell, splintered into the shape of a cross. Scattered around his feet are the keys, bolts, and locks of the gates he has just broken.

To the left, an old man with white hair kneels with his arms outstretched—Adam, the first man. To the right, a woman in deep red kneels in the same posture—Eve, the first woman. Behind them stand the righteous of Israel: Abraham, David, and the prophets, watching and waiting. And Jesus, in the center, is reaching down to grasp Adam and Eve and pull them up out of the place of the dead. This is the image of the resurrection that the East has guarded for us, and that we in the West have largely forgotten. It is the picture lurking just behind the strange passage we heard from the first letter of Peter this morning. A passage that, at first glance, doesn't sound like Easter at all.

It begins with the question of suffering for doing right, then moves to the death and resurrection of Christ. And right when you think you know where it is going, it swerves off into this strange passage: "Christ, having been put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the Spirit, went and made a proclamation to the spirits in prison." This icon depicts the traditional interpretation of that verse. Between the cross on Good Friday and the empty tomb of Easter Sunday, Jesus went somewhere. He did not simply stop existing. He went where the dead go. He went down to those who had waited for centuries: Abraham, Sarah, Moses, David, Isaiah—all the righteous of Israel, waiting for the one whose coming they had long foretold. He proclaimed to them his victory over death and brought them home.

The church calls this the harrowing of hell. "Harrow" is an Old English word for a raid that strips a place bare. Christ harrows hell. He breaks down its gates, rescues its prisoners, and leaves the place empty. There is an ancient sermon on the harrowing of hell, traditionally read on Holy Saturday, that imagines the moment of the encounter between Christ and Adam. Christ enters holding his cross like a victorious weapon. Adam sees him, strikes his breast in terror, and calls out, "The Lord be with you." And Christ replies, "And also with you." This call and response that we've grown so accustomed to in this ancient telling began between Adam and his Savior in the realm of the dead.

Christ then takes Adam by the hand and says, "Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give you light. Arise, O man, work of my hands, arise, you who were fashioned in my image. Arise, let us go hence. For you in me and I in you, together we are one undivided person." This is what is happening in the icon. This is what Peter is talking about when he says Christ went and made a proclamation to the spirits in prison. The Lord went down to rescue his own. And among those spirits were Mary's own forebears: Abraham and Sarah, David and his line, her parents Joachim and Anne, the whole company of the patriarchs and matriarchs from whom she came. The genealogies in Matthew and Luke list them each by name. When Christ descended to the dead, he descended for them.

At the end of the mass today, we will crown the woman from whom this rescuer came. The Church has, from the earliest days, held Mary in singular honor because she is the only person in history who carried God in her body, and because Christ himself honored her by becoming her son. We do not worship her, as worship is due God alone. We honor her with a crown of flowers in this loveliest of months, as the woman whose body gave the rescuer the hands that grasp Adam in the icon, and the feet that stand on the broken gates of hell.

Peter has more to say, though. He tells us, almost in passing, that the same Christ who descended to the dead is the one whose work meets us at the font. "Baptism now saves you," he writes, "not the removal of dirt from the body, but an appeal to God for a good conscience through the resurrection of Jesus Christ." The harrowing of hell is not just a story that happened only once, long ago, to people we have never met. The harrowing of hell is what happens at every baptism. The same Christ who reaches down toward Adam reaches down toward the candidate at the font. The same waters that flooded the world in Noah's day, the same waters of the River Jordan that Christ himself was baptized in—those waters meet us at the font, and we are pulled up out of the waters by the same hand that pulled up Adam.

The harrowing of hell is not a doctrine for debate in the seminary classroom only. It is a word of hope to every one of us who has known some kind of prison. Some of us have known grief that felt like a tomb we could not climb out of. Some of us have known the death of someone we loved—a spouse, a parent, a child, a friend. And the question that haunted us was not whether God exists in the daylight with us, but whether God could reach the place where our loved ones had gone. Some of us have known despair, its own kind of hell, and have wondered whether God was listening to our cries for help.

The harrowing of hell is the apostolic answer to every one of those prisons. There is no place where Christ has not gone. There is no realm of grief he has not entered. There is no death from which he cannot lead us home. The patriarchs and matriarchs waited for thousands of years, and he came for them. The friend who took his own life is not beyond Christ's reach. The grief that has held you in its grip for months is not beyond his reach either. Jesus has been there, and he has come back. And Jesus, as we see in the icon, does not come back alone.

Dear friends, the Christ in this icon is not far away. He is not halfway around the world in Cyprus. He is not only in the past. He is here in this room, reaching down just as he did toward Adam and Eve and all of Mary's forebears, extending his hand of rescue to us in the bread of heaven and the cup of salvation.
