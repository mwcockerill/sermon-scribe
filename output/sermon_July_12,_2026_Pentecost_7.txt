In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Take a moment and look at the pew where you are sitting. Someone sat in this pew and knelt at the kneelers in front of you, praying for you, me, and those around us generations ago. The windows that colored this morning's light were a gift from people whose names most of us have forgotten. And the organ, the instrument that has supported our singing for generations, is failing now precisely because it has done its work so long and so faithfully. 

Nearly everything your eye rests on this morning is a harvest. It grew from seed that others scattered, and they scattered it not knowing the harvest that would come generations later. A sower went out to sow. That is how Jesus begins, sitting in a boat pushed out from the shore, the whole crowd standing on the beach. The first thing to notice about this sower is that he is, by any sensible measure, wasteful. He flings the seed everywhere. Some fall on the hard-packed path where the birds are waiting. Some fall on rocky ground with barely any soil, where it sprouts fast and dies even faster. Some fall among thorns that choke it. Only some fall on good earth. Three parts lost, one part saved. No careful farmer would sow this way. You would aim. You would protect your seed. He would not throw it onto the road. 

But this sower is not being careful. He is being generous—recklessly, almost scandalously generous—casting the word of the kingdom onto ground that offers no promise at all. That is the heart of today's parable. He sows on the hard path anyway. He sows among the rocks anyway. He does not first inspect the soil to see whether it deserves the seed. The sower scatters everywhere generously because that is the kind of sower he is. St. John Chrysostom, preaching on this parable 1,600 years ago, noted that with ordinary seed and ordinary ground, this would be folly because a rock cannot turn itself into soil, and a footpath cannot decide to become a field. 

But with human souls, it is different, he says. A hardened heart can soften. Shallow ground can deepen. The sower keeps casting seed on the unpromising places precisely because, unlike a farmer, he is not wrong to hope that the ground will change. Well, that should be a relief to each of us because, if we are honest, we are not only one kind of soil; we can be all of them. There is hard-packed path in me, places worn smooth and unreceptive by long bad habits. There is rocky ground where I receive a word with joy at church on Sunday but have lost it by Tuesday. There are thorns—the cares and worries that crowd in and choke what was starting to grow. And there is, by the grace of God, good soil too. 

Jesus is not sorting us into the fruitful and the failed. He is describing the mixed ground of an ordinary human heart, the same heart the sower has never once stopped tending. And then we hear how the story ends. Other seeds fell on good soil and brought forth grain, sometimes a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty. In the world Jesus is describing, this is not a good year. This is a staggering, almost unbelievable yield, far beyond anything a farmer could reasonably expect. The parable spends three lines on failure and ends on abundance, out of all proportion to the loss. 

Each of us is prone to measure our lives by what we can see slipping away. We count the seed on the path, we lament the waste, and tally our losses. We look at what is hard and rocky and thorny in ourselves and in our common life, and we can grow discouraged. But thanks be to God, the kingdom of God is not measured by what is lost along the way. It is measured by the harvest that God gives, and that harvest is disproportionate, extravagant—a hundredfold return that no farmer could expect. The increase is not up to us; it is God's to give. 

Which brings us back to your pew. Everything holy in this room was sown by people who trusted that God gives the harvest. They couldn't see the harvest, and yet they scattered anyway. The faithful who gathered in a rented butcher shop on Bryn Mawr Avenue in 1888, who could smell the sawdust and the blood and still knew that God was in their midst. The ones who gave the windows, the mosaics, the statue of the Blessed Mother. The ones who gave and tended to the pipe organ. They sowed a harvest they would never fully reap. We are reaping it this morning. We are their hundredfold. 

And in a few moments, we will do the very thing they did, week after week, month after month, on thousands of successive Sundays. We will come to this altar just as we are—mixed ground, hard and rocky and thorny, and yes, fertile. The Great Sower will not try to sort all of that out at the altar rail. He will give us Himself, His own body and blood, generously, almost recklessly, into hands and mouths that don't deserve such amazing grace. Grace that then empowers us to scatter the seed of the Word with the same scandalous generosity with which it was scattered in our hearts, to trust that God will bring about a bountiful harvest that we could never expect.
