Growing up along the Gulf Coast of Florida, we spent a lot of time around boats. There is something about boats that can make us feel safe. A boat gives us something solid beneath our feet. It creates boundaries between us and the chaos of the water. Even in the middle of a storm, a boat feels more secure than open waters. I suspect we all have our own kind of boats. They are the places where life feels familiar and predictable. They are our routines, our assumptions, our communities, our political tribes, our carefully formed opinions, and even our churches. These are not bad. In fact, the disciples' boat, just like ours, carries us through difficult seasons.

But today's gospel asks a question that is far more challenging than, "Do you have enough faith to walk on water?" It asks, "Are you willing to leave the boat when Jesus calls?" That is a more uncomfortable question. The disciples are in the middle of a storm; the wind is against them, the waves are crashing, they are exhausted and afraid, and they are in the darkness. They see someone walking toward them on the sea. Their first reaction is not hope; it is fear. "It is a ghost!" How often fear distorts our vision. How easy it is to sometimes mistake God's presence for yet another threat.

Jesus immediately speaks to their fear: "Take heart; it is I. Be not afraid." He does not calm the storm first; he speaks first. Only later does the wind become still. What an important lesson of faith! God does not always remove the storm before inviting us into trust. Instead, Christ meets us in the middle of it. Peter, being Peter, responds in the only way he knows how: "Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you." And Jesus answers with one word: "Come." That one word changes everything. Peter climbs over the side of the boat and begins walking to Jesus.

Now, Peter is often remembered as the disciple who sank, but I think he should be remembered as the disciple who got out of the boat. Eleven others stayed exactly where they were. They remained safe. They remained relatively dry. But Peter was the only one who discovered what it was like to stand where Jesus stood. Before he sank, he walked. But the miracle is not that Peter walked on water; the miracle is that he trusted Jesus enough to step into the unknown. That, I think, is what discipleship looks like—not certainty, but participation. Following Jesus has never simply been about believing the right things. It has always been about joining Christ where Christ is already at work. And that always involves risk.

That is true throughout scripture. Elijah discovers this in our first reading. He has fled into the wilderness, convinced that he is alone. He hides in a cave, exhausted and afraid. God tells him to come out. Then comes the wind, the earthquake, the fire—powerful, dramatic displays that seem like the obvious place to find God. But God isn't in any of them. Instead, Elijah encounters God in what scripture beautifully calls "the sound of sheer silence." God's voice is quieter than fear, quieter than chaos, quieter than despair. But Elijah has to leave the cave before he can hear it. Peter has to leave the boat before he can experience it. Faith often begins with stepping beyond the places where we feel protected.

I wonder if this is especially important for us to hear today. We are living in a culture that is shaped by fear. Every day we are told what to fear next: violence, economic uncertainty, immigrants, change, disease, decline. Fear has become one of the strongest forces shaping our common life. We carry it in our conversations, in our news feeds, in our politics, and sometimes, yes, even into our churches and our very bodies. Some of those fears arise from real pain and genuine uncertainty, and we should never dismiss them lightly. And yet, fear has a way of shrinking our world. It teaches us that safety is our highest calling, but the gospel says otherwise.

The gospel says our highest calling is not self-protection; it is participation. Participation in the life of Christ, participation in the work of reconciliation, participation in mercy, in justice, in compassion, in hope. And all of these things require us to step beyond ourselves. Sometimes we ask the wrong question in the church. We often ask, "How do we keep the boat afloat?" It's not a bad question, but I think that Jesus is asking another one: "Who is willing to come out here?" Because if we pay attention to the Gospels, Jesus rarely stays where it is comfortable. He is constantly crossing boundaries. He goes toward the sick, toward the stranger, toward the outsider, toward the forgotten, toward everyone else who is avoided.

If we truly believe that Christ is alive and active in the world, then perhaps we should expect to find him there—not only inside these walls, but beside the lonely and among the poor, in neighborhoods longing for peace, across lines of race and politics and ideology, in difficult conversations, in acts of forgiveness, in places where love costs us something. Christ is already there. The question is whether we are willing to follow.

Of course, stepping out always carries the possibility of sinking; Peter reminds us of that. The moment he hears the wind, his fear returns, and he begins to sink. But notice carefully what happens next. Jesus does not lecture him. Jesus does not shame him. Jesus reaches for him. Perhaps that is the greatest miracle of the story—not that Peter walked on water, but that when Peter's courage gave way to fear, Christ's love did not. The hand of Jesus is quicker than Peter's failure. And perhaps that is why we can risk stepping out in the first place. Our confidence is not in our ability to walk on water; our confidence is in the one who never lets us drown.

In a few moments, we will gather around this table where Christ meets us again. He feeds us, he strengthens us, and he reminds us that we belong to him. But the Eucharist is never the end of the journey; it is our launching place. We are fed here so that we can go where Christ is already walking—into our neighborhoods, our workplaces, into places of hurt and healing, into conversations that require courage. Because the Church was never called to build stronger boats. The Church was called to become a people courageous enough to follow Jesus wherever he leads. And if we find ourselves sinking from time to time—and we will—we need not despair, for the same hand that reached for Peter still reaches for us. Amen.
