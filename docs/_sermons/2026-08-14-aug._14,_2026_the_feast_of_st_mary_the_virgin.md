In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Many of you did it when you came in without even thinking about it. You came down the aisle, and before you took your seat, you paused. You bent a knee or bowed your head toward the place where the Blessed Sacrament is reserved, right here in the middle of the high altar. Some of you learned this gesture as children. Some of you learned it here by watching others and mimicking their actions. Either way, you have done it so many times that it is routine.

We call that place the Tabernacle. The word means "tent," and it reaches back past this building, past the Reformation, past the Middle Ages, past Jesus, to a portable tent in the desert where the God who made all things decided to camp among a wandering people. At the heart of that tent was a wooden box overlaid with gold, containing the tablets of the covenant—the Ark of the Covenant, the place where heaven touched earth. Israel carried it into battle. David danced in front of it. A man named Uzzah reached out to steady it when the oxen stumbled, and he died on the spot because it turns out that the presence of God is not a thing you can casually put your hand on.

We bow or genuflect to the Tabernacle in our day because we believe the same God is in it. In our Gospel reading this evening, Mary, newly pregnant, sets out from Nazareth and travels into the hill country of Judah. It is a walk of several days over rough ground, undertaken by a young woman in the first weeks of a pregnancy she has not yet explained. She arrives at the house of Zechariah and greets her cousin Elizabeth. Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, cries out, "And why has this happened to me, that the mother of my Lord comes to me?" Centuries before, King David set out to bring the Ark of the Covenant up into the hill country of Judah. When the holiness of the thing frightened him, he asked, "How can the Ark of the Lord come to me?" 

Luke's audience has this context in mind when they hear the Gospel reading. Mary arose and went into the hill country just as David did. Elizabeth asks David's question, and the Ark, when David could not bring it any further, stayed three months in the house of Obed-Edom and blessed that house—exactly how long Mary stayed with her cousin. And there is the leaping. When Mary's voice reaches Elizabeth's ear, the child in her womb leaps. The word Luke uses is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for mountains skipping at the approach of God and for calves let out into the spring. It is the word for what David did in front of the Ark when he danced before the Lord with all his might. John the Baptist does in his mother's womb what David did in the streets of Jerusalem—dances because the Ark has come into the room. Except that this Ark is a teenage girl.

The old Ark had no say in the matter, but this one was asked, and she said yes. Inside the old Ark were the tablets of the law, a jar of old manna, and Aaron's rod that budded a flower. Inside this one is the one who spoke the law, the bread that came down from heaven, and the great high priest. The old Ark had golden cherubim spread over it, wings touching. The Church has sung for centuries that this one is higher than the cherubim and more glorious than the seraphim. This is the feast we keep today—a feast of a God who consented to be contained first in a box of gold laid in wood, and then in a girl from Nazareth.

In a few moments, on this altar, the bread and the wine will become the body and blood of Christ, and you will come forward and hold out your hands or open your mouth, and I will place Him there. You will eat and drink, and then you'll walk back to your pew. The Church Fathers had a Greek word for what you become in that moment: Christophoros, Christ-bearer. You will be what Mary was—a living tabernacle, a tent God has consented to camp in, moving through the world with the glory inside of you.

So when you exit your pew after Mass and do the same routine thing of bowing or genuflecting toward the Tabernacle, remember you become what you receive in the most holy sacrament of the altar. Each time you receive communion, you are conformed a little more into the likeness of Jesus Christ. And each time you receive communion, you carry the God who created all things in your body into the streets of Chicago, into your week, into the difficult conversation you have been avoiding, into the ordinary routine of life. 

The old Ark held three signs of what God had done: His word cut into stone, a jar of bread from the wilderness, and a dead stick that had bloomed. Mary carried the one they were all pointing to, and so will you. May God, by that grace, remind you of who you are becoming. Amen. We believe in the Lord.
