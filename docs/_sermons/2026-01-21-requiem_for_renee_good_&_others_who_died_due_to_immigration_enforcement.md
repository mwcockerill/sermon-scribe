In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, we gather this evening in the face of death—not death in the abstract, but death that has a name and a face. We come to commend to Almighty God Renee Nicole Good, and with her, all those whose lives have been taken by the machinery of immigration enforcement. We come carrying grief, anger, confusion, and fear. Like Martha standing before Jesus at the tomb of her brother, many of us may be praying words we barely dare to say out loud: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." The Church does not discourage that prayer; she teaches us how to pray it in a requiem mass like this one. Jesus does not rebuke Martha for her grief, nor does he try to explain it away. He meets her in the midst of it. He doesn't offer a timeline, nor a theory of the afterlife, nor a reassurance that everything will somehow make sense later. He offers himself: "I am the resurrection and the life."

Death always tries to tell its own story. The powers that killed Renee Nicole Good tell a story too. They call it law enforcement, they call it procedure, they call it order. But the Church is not bound to accept the state's moral framing of its own actions. Whatever legal language may be used, what happened here was unjust. Human beings, created in the image of God, were treated as problems to be managed rather than neighbors to be loved. The Gospel does not permit us to call that justice, no matter how orderly some claim it is. Rome spoke the same way when Jesus was crucified—legally, efficiently, and with public justification. The crucifixion teaches us that legality and justice are not always the same thing. Jesus was killed without breaking the law; he was killed by the law. And God's verdict on that killing was not silence, but resurrection. The Gospel insists that death does not get the final word about itself.

The cross of Jesus Christ exposes the lie at the heart of every empire that claims the right to decide which lives are expendable. At a prayer vigil last week, Bishop Craig Loya of Minnesota named this truth with clarity and courage. He said of the early Christians, "They turned the world upside down by mobilizing for love—not by being stronger than the empire, not by matching its violence, but by refusing to believe that cruelty was inevitable or that death was sovereign." That same refusal is demanded of us now—not as a catchy slogan, but as a way of life grounded in the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

At a requiem mass such as this one, the Church speaks with unusual honesty. We name death for what it is. We mourn. We entrust the dead to God's mercy because none of us stands without that mercy. And still, we proclaim that Renee's life has ultimately changed, not ended. I recently learned that before she moved to Minneapolis, Renee worshiped as part of Edgewater Presbyterian Church, just down the street at Bryn Mawr and Kenmore. Renee was a human being made in God's image and an active member of the body of Christ right here in Chicago. Tonight, we commend her into the merciful arms of her Lord and Savior—the same Lord who knows what it is to be arrested, imprisoned, and executed unjustly, and who now holds her in his mercy.

St. Paul says, "Death has been swallowed up in victory." He does not say that death has been explained away, justified, glossed over, or softened. He says that it has been swallowed up, overwhelmed by a power greater than itself. The resurrection of Jesus is God's verdict on a world that kills in the name of order. Love wins, and death loses. And yet, death still feels very much present. The fear is real, the violence has not stopped, and the weariness is deep. The Church does not pretend otherwise. Instead, she places bread and wine on the altar and dares to say that here, in the midst of grief, rage, and exhaustion, the risen Christ gives himself to us.

This is not an escape from the world's suffering. To proclaim resurrection in a world like ours is not neutral; it is an act of resistance. The Gospel does not allow the Church to make peace with injustice, even when that injustice is lawful, normalized, or politically convenient. We resist not because we are partisan, but because we are baptized. From this promise of resurrection, we are given the strength to stand, to endure, and to remain faithful. To receive the Eucharist tonight is to make a dangerous confession. It is to say that we will not meet hatred with hatred, nor violence with violence. It is to say that we will not allow our anger to be consumed by the very forces that produced this death. It is to say that we belong to a Lord whose victory looks like a cross, whose throne is an altar, and whose power is made perfect in selfless, unconditional love.

So tonight, we commend Renee Nicole Good and all those killed in the name of immigration enforcement to the mercy of God. We pray for the repose of their souls. We grieve with those who mourn, and then, nourished by this sacrament, we go forth—not naïve, not passive, and not resigned, but anchored in the unshakable truth that Jesus Christ is risen from the dead.
