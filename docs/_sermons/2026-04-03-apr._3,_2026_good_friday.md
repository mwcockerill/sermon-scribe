I thought I was okay with death until I experienced my first unexpected and tragic death of a young woman while working as a chaplain at Rush Hospital last summer, part of my training for the priesthood. I had lost people in my life: grandparents, classmates, the inexplicable death of an aunt at 50, and my father-in-law. I also worked as a church musician from the time I was 13 years old, playing for countless funerals and memorial services. In fact, I remember one Friday night when I was called to play the piano at three different memorial services, realizing this was definitely not the typical teenage experience of a Friday night.

Because of my proximity to death and my self-perceived ability to handle it well, I somewhat naively chose the intensive care unit and hospice unit at Rush. I figured that as a hopeful parish priest, I wanted and needed the experience of sitting with death, both with patients and their families. I quickly realized how many deaths occur in a hospital every day, how surprising, sweeping, harrowing, and indiscriminate death is, but also how sacred it can be. Young, old, black, white, addict, rich, poor, unhoused, CEO—death does not discriminate.

I also realized that my natural tendency is to fix, to try and help others and myself escape pain. It took me an entire summer to learn that it wasn't my job to make the living or the dying feel better with my words or actions. Words and actions that, no matter how well-intentioned or coming from a place of deep sincerity, did not, could not take the sting and pain of death away. Words that ultimately rang hollow. I needed to just be—to be present, to listen, to sit, to watch, to laugh and to cry, to pray and not to pray, to see myself and those I love and the patients that died that summer, to honestly confront and know that one day it will be my turn.

The gospel writers have differing accounts of who was present with Jesus at his hour of death. The evangelists give us varying levels of specificity with the names, but all four of the gospels particularly highlight the presence of women at the foot of the cross. Women who had followed Jesus, who had provided for Jesus—women who, though Matthew, Mark, and Luke state might have looked on or stood at a distance, nonetheless were there. Women who witnessed Jesus' suffering, who were present and stayed despite the horror that was happening to the Son of God before their eyes. Women who knew that they couldn't fix this or do anything to relieve his or their own suffering.

Their devotion to Jesus, their presence with him at his most humiliating and traumatic hour, is so important to us tonight because of just how monumental the death of Jesus is. Not only is this a cornerstone of our faith, but also in its scope, it literally changed the course of the whole world. The catechism, or question-and-answer summary of the doctrines of the Episcopal Church, asks: What is the great importance of Jesus' suffering and death? The answer is: By his obedience, even to suffering and death, Jesus made the offering which we could not make. In him, we are freed from the power of sin and reconciled to God.

Jesus' death reminds us that our salvation is not based on anything we can do or fix—not on our works, our gifts, our talents, or our devotion to God—but because of the death of Jesus. We are called tonight to sit and to be present with how great of a sacrifice and gift this truly is. To be present for the realization of just how massive the scope of this act of love is for humanity. That we would no longer live for ourselves but for him who died and was raised for us.

Our modern culture does a pretty good job of helping us to avoid confronting death. Life in the West in the 21st century often equates to doing everything we can to prolong, avoid, and hide death. This was not the case in the ancient world and even in our culture as recently as a hundred years ago, where death was much more present. We now live in a digital age, all consumed by media, of which the main goal seems to be to take us out of the present, out of facing what we're feeling. We turn to distraction to numb or fix our pain and our boredom. Time passes so quickly because we're often just getting through the present, thinking it will lead us to a supposedly better present. And the cycle continues.

Tonight, we are called not to fix, not to distract, not to numb, not to skip over to the end, not to think of bright flowers and bright colors and celebratory meals. But tonight, my friends, we sit and remain present with the pain and the suffering and the death of our Lord. We sit with those who experienced it, especially the women at the foot of the cross: Jesus' mother Mary, Mary's sister, and Mary Magdalene, along with the countless unnamed women who had the courage to face and sit with Jesus in his death. 

Tonight, we sit not only with the death of Jesus but with those we have had the privilege of knowing, loving, and losing. I encourage you to not be afraid to sit with this discomfort, this pain, and grief and mourning—to try not to fix, to look not only at the mortality of Jesus but also your own. We have the courage and the strength as Christians to do this because, in the words of St. Paul, tonight we are afflicted in every way but not crushed; we are perplexed but not driven to despair; persecuted but not forsaken; struck down but not destroyed. Because death hasn't won and will never win.
