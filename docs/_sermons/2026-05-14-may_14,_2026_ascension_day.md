In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. We are afraid. Not all of us in the same way and not all of us for the same reasons. But there is, across this congregation and across the country, a low and steady fear about what is happening in our common life. Some of us are afraid for family and friends who feel unsafe. Some of us are afraid because institutions we thought were trustworthy actually aren't. Some of us are afraid in ways we cannot quite name. 

Many of us have learned over the past several years that the powers of this age are larger, hungrier, and less restrained than we had thought possible. Here again what St. Paul prays for the saints at Ephesus: that the eyes of their hearts would be enlightened to see the immeasurable greatness of his power for us who believe according to the working of his great power. God put this power to work in Christ when he raised him from the dead and seated him at his right hand in the heavenly places, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion, and above every name that is named, not only in this age but in the age to come. 

Paul is straining at the edges of the Greek language. Rule, authority, power, dominion. He keeps reaching for more words because the lordship of the risen Christ exceeds what any one of them alone can carry. This is not measured prose; this is a coronation hymn for the ascended Christ. And the question for us tonight, as we stand under that hymn, is whether we believe it. The early Christians certainly did, and they died for it. The simplest baptismal confession in the New Testament is two Greek words: Kyrios, Jesus—Jesus is Lord. In the Roman world, that confession was politically dangerous because the parallel confession demanded by the empire was Kyrios, Caesar—Caesar is Lord. The two could not coexist. 

When the Roman magistrate told the elderly Polycarp of Smyrna in the second century to renounce Christ and swear allegiance to Caesar to save his life, Polycarp replied, "Eighty-six years have I served him, and he has done no wrong. How can I then blaspheme my king and my savior?" They burned him alive, and he went to the fire singing. What St. Polycarp understood, and what the church has had to relearn in every generation, is that the lordship of Christ over the powers of the age is not just a theory. It is a confession that orders our lives and puts us at odds with every claim to ultimate allegiance other than the one we make at this altar. 

This is why a small group of German pastors and theologians in May of 1934 gathered in a town called Barmen to write what they called a declaration. The German Christian movement had fallen in step with the Nazi state. Most of the German church was following right along with them. The confessing church, led by Karl Barth, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Martin Niemöller, and others, refused. The first article of their declaration reads, "Jesus Christ, as he is attested to in the Holy Scripture, is the one word of God whom we have to hear and whom we have to trust and obey in life and in death. We reject the false doctrine that the Church could and should recognize as a source of its proclamation beyond and besides this one word of God, yet other events, powers, historic figures, and truths as God's revelation." 

That is Kyrios Iesous in 20th-century German, and that is Polycarp's defiance of the emperor. The question is never whether the powers of this age will demand our allegiance, for they always will. The question is whether we will give it to them. Every age has its Caesars. Every age has its German Christians who fall in line, and the temptation cuts in every direction. To think that the right election result will save us. To think that the wrong election result is damnation. To think that the cause we believe in, however just, is identical with the kingdom of God. 

Whatever your politics, whatever side you imagine yourself to be on tonight, hear this: Jesus Christ is not a partisan member of your political tribe. Christ is Lord above every tribe. Christ is Lord above the political opponent you fear. Christ is Lord above this country, above the Church, above the powers you love and the powers you hate. He has been seated at the right hand of the Father, far above all rule and authority and power and dominion. There is no other Lord. 

And here's where the joy of this great feast meets the anxiety we are faced with. The disciples in Luke's gospel do not return to Jerusalem in despair after watching their Lord ascend; they return with great joy. They worship. They go to the temple and bless God. They have understood finally what the Father has done. The crucified one has been enthroned. The one the empire executed has been vindicated above every earthly power. Their joy is not denial. They are not pretending that Rome is benign. They are not blind to what had just happened to their Lord. They are joyful because they have seen what the Father did with the empire's verdict: He overturned it. 

And so they look at one another in a hostile city under Roman occupation, and they are not afraid. They are joyful because they know who reigns. Look at the Paschal candle. The flame that has burned in our midst since Easter has been put out. The ritual of extinguishing the candle on Ascension Day is nearly 900 years old, and the smoke has just ascended, as Christ's body did on that first Ascension Day. But just as he promised, he did not leave us orphaned. By the power of the Holy Spirit, he abides with us in a different way, in the most holy sacrament of the altar and in the hearts of all his faithful people. 

The candle has been extinguished because the light has been handed to you. In the midst of your fears about what is happening in the world, you will leave this place as bearers of the paschal fire. You will carry it into your workplace, your neighborhood, your voting booth, your conversation with frightened friends, your prayers for your enemies, your refusal to bow to the powers of this world. And those powers are real. They are frightening, but they have been put under the feet of the one who is seated above every name in heaven, on earth, and under the earth. Like the disciples at Bethany, when we look at the world's fear, may we respond with confidence and with joy.
