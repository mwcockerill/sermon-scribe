Life is hard. The passage we heard from St. Paul's letter to the Romans includes a verse that is often used to address the fact that life is hard. The New Revised Standard Version translates verse 28: "We know that all things work together for good for those who love God." There's a small footnote to this verse in which we are given a different translation that some scholars prefer: "God makes all things work together for good." 

All things working together for good is different than God making all things work together for good. Throughout this chapter of Romans, God is indeed the subject. It is God who actively works to redeem all of creation. It is God's Spirit who helps us in our weakness. It is God's Spirit who intercedes for us according to God's will. The traditional translation of this verse implies that somehow human suffering is good, or that if we would only love God enough, everything will be alright. But Paul doesn't begin there. He says that the Spirit helps us in our weakness, for we do not know how to pray as we ought, but that the very Spirit intercedes with sighs too deep for words. 

Paul does not say that the Spirit teaches us to pray better or gives us the right words. He says that when we have no words at all, when the grief is too raw, when the fear is too overwhelming, when we sit down to pray and we feel alone, God the Holy Spirit is already praying on our behalf. Underneath our silence, beneath the words we cannot find, the Spirit is interceding for us, and God who searches the heart understands that wordless prayer perfectly. Life is indeed hard, and sometimes it's so hard that we can't even bring ourselves to pray. And that is precisely where Paul begins, not with our strength, but with God's Spirit groaning within us on our behalf. 

Life is hard. No matter how much we love God or how pious we are, or how many times we go to Mass, no matter how many cans of food we donate to the food pantry, no matter how hard we try, we cannot be good enough to deserve God's love. It is God who makes all things work together for good. And thank God for that, because life in our parish this week has been hard. We buried two beloved parishioners. I can think of at least four who are too sick to be with us this morning and would give anything to be in the pew next to you. Two others have recently lost their jobs. Some among us are wrestling with doubt, wondering where God is in all of it. Others are in chronic pain, pain that is so intense it affects all aspects of their lives. 

This is the world St. Paul is writing into—not a world in which we can try hard enough or pray hard enough and poof, all our suffering goes away, but a world where grief, sickness, and fear abound. And into that world, Paul says, it is God who makes all things work together for our good. For he predestined those who loved him to be conformed to the image of his Son. The word "predestined" does not mean that God overpowers our free will, for our free will is a gift from God. John of Damascus says, "God does not will evil to be done, nor does he force virtue upon us." This term "predestined" doesn't apply to us as individuals, but rather means that God has a specific calling for his people from the beginning of the world. And that calling is to be conformed into the image of his Son. 

We know that Jesus suffered a horrible death on the cross on that first Good Friday. Being conformed into Christ's image means that we too will suffer. But just as Jesus was raised from the dead three days later, so too will we be redeemed from our present sufferings. Life is hard. Being a Christian doesn't change that. It doesn't mean that you'll always get the promotion you think you deserve. It doesn't mean that your marriage will be easy. It doesn't mean that if you pray hard enough, the pain will go away. Being conformed into the image of Jesus means that we will suffer, but thanks be to God, it also means that we will be redeemed. 

Paul then proceeds to make his point using a question-and-answer format. These questions have simple answers, and after gaining some momentum, he asks the ultimate question he has in mind: "Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will hardship or distress or persecution or famine or nakedness or peril or sword?" Paul had experienced all of these things in his lifetime except for the sword. No, he says, "In all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us." As if his first list of difficult situations wasn't enough, he gives us another list of things that cannot separate us from God's love: death, rulers, things present, things to come, powers. In other words, all the things that we fear the most. 

Dear friends, life is hard. Who will separate us from the love of God in Christ? Will hardship or distress or peril or job loss or pain or marital troubles? No. Nothing can separate us from the love of God, not even death itself. As you and I are frustrated, scared, and overwhelmed, we can rest on this beautiful promise. No matter what happens in the world around us, and no matter how often we sin and fall short of God's glory, we can never be separated from the love of God. Paul says earlier in verse 32, "God did not withhold his only Son but gave him up for us all. Will he not with him also give us everything?" 

God's love is by its very nature self-sacrificial. The God who gave up his only Son to keep us in his love will not let us go now. And in a moment, that same self-offering will be made present on this altar. The Son who was given up for us gives himself to us again under the forms of bread and wine—a pledge that nothing in all creation can separate us from his love. We come to the altar rail with two funerals behind us, with the sick and the grieving among us, with our own fears, doubts, and pain, and God meets us in the midst of our suffering by giving us himself. 

Life is hard, but God makes all things work together for our good. Come to the altar and receive the love that will not let you go. Amen.
