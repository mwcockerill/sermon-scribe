In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.

A few weeks ago, I had a midday meeting close to Wrigley Field, not knowing that it was opening day for the Cubs. As I made my way down the sidewalk, I came across a group of young Cubs fans who had clearly been preparing for the game at a bar for some time. One of them spotted my collar, and his face lit up. He begged me, "Father, could you please bless the Cubbies? We really want them to win." Well, I didn't have the heart to tell him in the moment that it doesn't quite work that way. So, much to their delight, I blessed them and went on about my day.

Well, if the priesthood doesn't work that way, what is the priesthood, and why does it matter for any of us? In our second lesson today, St. Peter writes to a small, scattered, often suffering group of new Christians in what is now Turkey. Most of them are Gentile converts. They have just been baptized, and they are wondering who they are now in a world suspicious of them, sometimes to the point of persecution. Peter tells them, "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own people, in order that you may proclaim the mighty acts of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light."

A royal priesthood. He doesn't say that some of you are royal priests. He doesn't say that the ordained leaders among you are a royal priesthood. He says, "You, every baptized one of you." Saint Peter is reaching back to a moment in Israel's history from the foot of Mount Sinai. Before God gave them the Ten Commandments, before he established the priesthood of Aaron, before he commanded the building of the tabernacle, he spoke to all of Israel through Moses and said, "You shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation." Every Israelite, every man, woman, and child was to be called a priest, to stand between God and the world, to mediate God's blessing to the nations. Peter takes that ancient promise and applies it now to the baptized, to you, to me, to the person sitting next to you in the pew. By the waters of baptism, you have been made a priest.

St. Augustine, preaching some 400 years later, put it like this: "Just as we call all Christians Christ's because all of us have been anointed in baptism, so we call all Christians priests because all of us in our baptism have been made offerers of the one sacrifice of Jesus Christ." I'm often asked why we Episcopalians call our ministers priests when essentially no other Protestant denomination does. The short answer is this: priests offer sacrifices. That is what a priest has always done in every religious culture throughout human history—the one who stands at an altar and offers a sacrifice to God. The Levitical priests of the Old Testament offered sacrifices of bulls, goats, and lambs. The pagan priests of Greece and Rome offered the sacrifices appropriate to their cult. And Christian priests, since the apostolic age, have offered the one sacrifice of Jesus Christ at the altar in the consecrated bread and wine of the Eucharist.

We call our ministers priests because the central act of their ministry is to stand at an altar and offer a sacrifice. By your baptism, you too are called to offer sacrifices to God. Not the bloody animal sacrifices of the Old Covenant, not the sacramental sacrifices of the church, but what Peter calls spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ. The sacrifice of praise when you sing a hymn or say your prayers. The sacrifice of your body when you offer your daily work as an act of worship. The sacrifice of mercy when you feed the hungry or sit with someone who is dying. The sacrifice of intercession when you pray for someone whose name only you remember. These are real priestly acts made acceptable to God through the one eternal sacrifice of Christ.

But there's also a sacramental priesthood, a particular priesthood within the priestly people of God, just as there was a particular priesthood of Aaron within the nation of Israel. This is the priesthood into which I was ordained. In most priestly ordinations in our church, at one point in the liturgy, the candidate prostrates themselves, lying face down on the floor while the litany for ordinations is chanted over them. If you were at the Good Friday liturgy a few weeks back, you saw the three sacred ministers prostrating themselves before the altar on the day on which our Lord was crucified and died. To be ordained a priest is to be configured in a special way to the crucified Christ. It is to consent to lay yourself down, even to die, for the people you are called to serve.

St. John Chrysostom wrote that the priest stands at the altar surrounded by angels who tremble at what is taking place, and that those who hold so great a charge ought to tremble themselves. When someone comes to me and expresses that they feel called to be ordained a priest, I always tell them early in their discernment that the collar is restricting. There are countless places that laypeople can go and countless ways they can serve that simply aren't possible for those of us who wear a collar. A lay Christian can hold almost any job in the world. An ordained priest cannot. A layperson can sit at a bar or attend a party without being the priest in the room. An ordained priest cannot. A layperson can be the friend at the bedside of someone dying whom no priest will ever be called for. A layperson can share God's love in a thousand places I can never enter on official business.

The ordained priesthood is a particular calling with a particular set of constraints. It is not a higher or holier form of Christian life. It is one form in service to the whole church. My priesthood and yours are not in competition with one another. The place where they meet, where they become visible together, is right here at the altar. In a few moments, I will stand there and say, "We offer our sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving." That "we" is not a royal "we"; it is all of us who are baptized. The bread and wine I will lift before God represent not only Christ's self-offering but yours—your work this past week, your sorrows, your joys, your prayers, your bodies, your very selves, joined by these human hands that were anointed with holy oil, joined to the one sacrifice of Christ.

At the end of the Eucharistic prayer, when you respond "Amen," that "Amen" is your priestly act. You are affirming and claiming as your own that offering of Christ. The ordained priesthood does not replace your priesthood; it gathers it up and joins it to the once-for-all sacrifice of Jesus. In our gospel today, Jesus tells his disciples, "Very truly I tell you, the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do, and in fact will do greater works than these." Greater works. Not because we are greater than Jesus, but because by the Holy Spirit, His ministry is now extended through every baptized person, in every place, in every time—the hands and feet of Christ multiplied millions of times over around the world.

And so, dear friends, go forth and do these greater works. Go to the colleague whose marriage is unraveling and offer the sacrifice of your listening ear. Go to the neighbor who has been told all her life that she does not belong and tell her by your welcome that she does. Pray for the people whose names will never appear in the prayers of the people, but whom you carry in your heart. The grace given to you at this altar confirms and strengthens the grace you received at the baptismal font and empowers you for these greater works. For you have been called out of darkness into his marvelous light. Once you were no people, and now you are God's people. Once you had not received mercy, but now you receive mercy.
