There is a line in that sequence hymn this morning that has been rolling around in my mind and heart all week. The hymn is a translation of the medieval Latin "Veni Sancte Spiritus," the golden sequence, likely by Archbishop of Canterbury Stephen Langton around the year 1200, and sung by the Western Church on this feast ever since. In the fourth stanza, the hymn names a series of things the Holy Spirit comes to do. The Spirit comes with soft rains to break our drought. The Spirit comes with rushing wind to shake our will. The Spirit comes with fire to melt our icy chill. And then this strange line: the Spirit comes to bring to light our perjuries.

It's an awfully strange thing to ask for. We don't normally come to church hoping to have our lives exposed. We come hoping to be comforted, strengthened, and drawn into the mystery of God here in the beauty of holiness. And yet the church, in her wisdom, has placed this petition at the heart of this hymn and asked us to sing it. The gospel we just heard tells us why. St. John's account of Pentecost is quieter than Luke's. There is no rushing wind, no tongues of fire, no multilingual praise. Instead, the frightened disciples are in a locked room. The risen Lord appears among them and shows them his wounds. And then he says, "Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, so I send you." 

Then he breathes on them. The Greek verb John uses here is rare; it appears nowhere else in the New Testament. It is the same verb the Greek Old Testament uses when God breathes the breath of life into Adam at the creation of the world. The risen Christ breathes on his disciples and says, "Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them. If you retain the sins of any, they are retained." The Holy Spirit is breathed for the forgiveness of sins. This is the very first thing the risen Lord says about why he's giving us the Spirit: for the forgiveness of sins. 

And as it turns out, there cannot be forgiveness without truth-telling. You cannot be forgiven for what you will not name. You cannot be absolved of what you refuse to admit. And so the Spirit, who comes to forgive sins, must first bring our perjuries to light. That is what the author of this hymn understood. The Spirit's work of forgiveness begins with honesty. The word "perjury" is a strong word. It means more than a casual untruth; a perjury is a lie told under oath, a falsehood sworn as if it were the truth. The word is not in the original Latin text of the hymn; it is the choice of the translator. The Latin original says something starker still: without the Spirit's power, nothing in us is innocent. 

The translator heard in that line all the lies we have sworn to and gave them a more poignant name: perjuries. And I think he was right. The most dangerous lies are not the ones we tell other people; they are the ones we tell ourselves over and over until we have built our lives on top of them and forgotten they were ever there. This is the work the Holy Spirit comes to do: to bring to light the things we have hidden from ourselves, the things we promised ourselves we would deal with someday but have quietly forgotten. The grudge we say we have let go but still dwell on in the wee hours of the night. The pattern of behavior we tell ourselves is harmless but have always known that it is not. The relationship we keep saying is fine but we really need to deal with. The addiction we keep saying is under control. The resentment we hold toward another person that we keep saying to ourselves is justified. 

The Holy Spirit shines on all of it—gently, persistently, mercifully. The Spirit brings our perjuries to light. And this work of the Spirit isn't just about each of us as individuals. The Spirit who exposes our private self-deceptions is the same Spirit who exposes the falsehood at the heart of empire. The same Spirit who descended on the Apostles on Pentecost Day sent them out into the world to tell the truth in a world that did not want to hear it. They went out and told the truth about Rome: that Caesar is not Lord. They told the truth about the temple authorities: that the man they handed over to die was truly the Messiah, the Son of God. And they told the truth about the social order: that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female. 

Saint Peter, who had perjured himself three times in the courtyard of the high priest, became the apostle who would not stop telling the truth. Dear friends, you and I need the same Holy Spirit now in our world today. We need the Spirit who brings perjuries to light—not only our private lies, but the public ones, the shared ones, the polite falsehoods by which a society agrees to live while some of its members are crushed. We need the Spirit to keep us honest. 

And so we come to this Mass this morning as those who need the Spirit's exposing light. Each of us has lies we have sworn to. Each of us has falsehoods we have built our lives on. Each of us is loved by a God whose mercy is more powerful than our self-deception. In a moment, as we do at nearly every Mass, we will confess our sins together. We will say, using words that most of us know by heart, that we have sinned against God in thought, word, and deed, by what we have done and by what we have left undone. 

And then I will pronounce the words of absolution: "Almighty God, have mercy on you. Forgive you all your sins through our Lord Jesus Christ. Strengthen you in all goodness, and by the power of the Holy Spirit, keep you in eternal life." Of course, our Protestant cousins are right that you can go directly to God with your confession and be forgiven. You can confess in the silence of your own heart and be forgiven, but the absolution pronounced by a priest or a bishop is a different thing altogether. It is the Church doing what the risen Lord empowered her to do when he breathed his Spirit on the disciples and said, "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven." 

I will proclaim to you the words of absolution with the Church's authority by the power of the Holy Spirit. We began with a strange prayer that the Spirit would bring our perjuries to light. It is not a prayer for the faint of heart, but it is the prayer of a people who trust that the God who exposes our lives does so in order to forgive us. And so let the light come. Let it fall on whatever you have hidden. And when you hear the words of absolution in a moment, do not ignore them as rote ritual that you have heard.
