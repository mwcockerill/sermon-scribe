In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen. Please be seated.

At the graveside, in hospital rooms, or late at night when the house is quiet, many prayers begin the same way: "Lord, if you had been there." If you had been here, the cancer would not have spread. If you had been here, the treatment would have worked. If you had been here, Natalie would still be with us today. This is the language of grief. This is the prayer of Martha when Jesus finally arrives in Bethany, and Lazarus is already four days dead. "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died."

The church in the Requiem Mass, such as this one, gives us permission to tell the truth about our grief in God's presence. Martha does not mince words. She doesn't say, "I know everything happens for a reason." She doesn't rush past her anger or disappointment. She names her loss. She names the ache she felt when God did not arrive on time. If we are honest, many of us feel exactly the same way today. Natalie was only 54 years old. She should have had more time. Omar and Imani should not have to outlive their mother. Annette should not have to bury her daughter-in-law. Janelle should not have to say goodbye to a friend who shared so much of her life. None of this feels right. And Martha teaches us that faith does not require pretending otherwise.

Notice what Jesus does not do. He doesn't correct Martha's theology. He doesn't tell her to be grateful for the time she had. He doesn't say that Lazarus is in a better place. He doesn't try to explain how all of this fits into God's mysterious plan. He lets her words of grief stand. Sometimes at funerals, we Christians jump immediately to the promise of resurrection in the hope that it will dull our pain. But before Jesus raises Lazarus, before he says a word about resurrection, he listens. Martha's faith is not fragile; it is honest. She still calls Jesus Lord. She still believes that God is at work. But she refuses to lie about her pain.

Cancer has a way of stripping away illusions. It doesn't negotiate. It doesn't respect schedules or family plans. It leaves people feeling exhausted, angry, and hopeful one moment and despairing the next. If you have walked this road with Natalie or someone you loved, you know how quickly easy answers can collapse. Martha's words meet us here, when polished theology is not enough. And then Jesus says something amazing. He doesn't point Martha to a doctrine. He doesn't say resurrection will happen at the last day. He says, "I am the resurrection and the life." Resurrection is not merely something that will happen at the end of time. It is a person who stands in front of grieving family members. It is a Savior who does not avoid cemeteries. It is a God who enters the place where hope already feels exhausted.

Even more astonishing is what comes next. A few verses later, though we didn't hear them read, we are told that Jesus weeps. He weeps because love always weeps when death intrudes. Friends, God does not observe our grief from a distance. God does not stand above this moment offering theological commentary. In Jesus Christ, God stands where we stand. God weeps where we weep. God knows what it is like to love and to lose. And that does not make Natalie's death any easier for us to accept. Resurrection hope does not make cancer less cruel. But it does mean that Natalie has been entrusted to a Savior who knows the cost of love. The same Jesus who wept at Lazarus' tomb is the one who holds her even now.

For Omar and Imani, this means that you do not have to choose between faith and anger. You can bring both of them to God. For Annette and Janelle and others, it means that your grief is not something to rush through or tidy up quickly. It is something Christ meets with you in the midst of your suffering. And for all of us, it means that the words, "Lord, if you had been here," are not the end of the prayer. They are just the beginning.

In a few moments, we will come to this altar. We will place bread and wine upon it, ordinary fragile things, and God will meet us here. Not with explanations, but with His presence. Not with answers, but with Himself. The Church teaches us that this sacrament is a foretaste of that heavenly banquet where there is no death, neither sorrow nor crying, but the fullness of joy. We do not leave our grief behind when we come forward. We bring it with us, and God meets us in the midst of it. This altar reaches farther than we can see. It stretches beyond this church, beyond this moment in time, even beyond the grave. Here, heaven and earth draw very near.

Natalie is not present to us as she once was, and that loss is real. But she is held securely in the risen life of Christ, who is Himself the resurrection and the life. And she celebrates with us at that heavenly banquet. The prophet Isaiah speaks of a day when God will swallow up death forever, and when the Lord will wipe away tears from every face. That promise does not deny our tears now. It tells us that they matter enough for God to wipe away with His own hand. Our sister Natalie lived a beautiful life, and because Christ is risen, death does not have the final word for her. Until that day of resurrection, we grieve, we mourn, we pray with honesty, and trust that love has vanquished death. May Natalie's soul and the souls of all the faithful departed, through the mercy of God, rest in peace.
