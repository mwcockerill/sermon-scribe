There is a small, easily overlooked phrase tucked into the angels' announcement to the two Marys at the tomb on that first Easter morning. The angel tells them that Jesus is not there, that he had been raised from the dead. And then he adds, almost as an aside, "as he said," for Jesus had told them that this would happen. Three times in Matthew's Gospel, he had told his disciples plainly, "The Son of Man will be handed over, crucified, and on the third day he will rise." Three times. And yet, when the moment arrived, not one of them was waiting at the tomb to see it, for they had not believed him.

The women came that morning expecting to grieve at a sealed tomb, not to encounter an angel sitting on top of it like he owned the place. As he said, I find myself returning to these three words this morning because they do something important. They tell us that the resurrection is not a plot twist, something God improvised when things went wrong on Good Friday. It is the completion of a story that Jesus himself was narrating from the beginning. The cross was not a tragedy that God had to recover from; it was the path, and the resurrection is where the path was always leading.

Matthew tells us that the angel's appearance was like lightning, his clothing white as snow. The guards posted to make absolutely sure nothing unusual happened shook with fear and fell to the ground as though dead. There's a certain irony in that the men sent to guard a corpse become, at the moment of the resurrection, as motionless as corpses themselves. But the angel turns to the women, and notice who they are. These are not the powerful, not the well-connected, not the ones with any standing in the religious establishment. They are two ordinary women named Mary, who had stayed by the cross when the disciples fled, who watched where the body was laid, who came back at dawn because they could not stay away. Their faithfulness in grief is the very thing that brings them to the empty tomb.

And the angel speaks directly to them: "Do not be afraid. I know that you are looking for Jesus who was crucified. He is not here, for he has been raised as he said. Come and see the place where he lay." It is an invitation to look, not to take someone else's word for it, not to accept a doctrine on faith before examining the evidence, but to come and see. The stone is rolled away not because the risen Christ needed a quick exit. The one who would later walk through locked doors was not inconvenienced by a sealed tomb. The stone is rolled away so that the women can look inside, so that we can look inside.

And this is a good day to do just that. We look inside on this day on which the church shouts from the rooftops that Jesus Christ rose bodily from the dead, not metaphorically, not spiritually, in some vague sense that his memory lives on or his teachings endure. The tomb was empty. The same body that was nailed to the cross, that was wrapped in linen and laid in that tomb, that body was raised, transformed and glorified. Yes, no longer subject to death, but a body so real, so present, that when the women encounter him on the road moments later, they grab his feet and will not let go.

I know this is not an easy thing to believe. Most of us in this room are educated people, and educated people know that dead bodies stay dead. A good friend who wasn't so churchy once sent me a text on Easter morning proclaiming, "Happy Zombie Jesus Day." My friend was not being cruel or rude; he was being honest, for he could not bring himself to believe that a human being could actually rise from the dead. But perhaps the empty tomb is not the final point in all of this. Perhaps the empty tomb is a door. And what is on the other side of it is not a verdict or a provable fact, but a person, running toward two frightened women on a road and speaking a word of joy. 

Fear and great joy both at once, not one and then the other, but simultaneously. I think Matthew is the most psychologically honest of the evangelists at this particular moment. Easter does not arrive as a warm, fuzzy feeling. It arrives as a disruption, something that happened that exceeds every category, that breaks every expectation, that cannot be tidied up into a simple Sunday school lesson. The women are both afraid and joyful because their world has been turned upside down. If you arrived here with settled, confident faith this morning, well, thanks be to God. And if you arrived with doubt or grief or even irritation, that is welcome here as well. For we approach the empty tomb as our authentic selves.

The angel only asks one thing: "Come." Not come with all the answers, not come with your doubts resolved, just come. Coming and seeing is never merely an intellectual exercise. What you find on the other side of that door is not a doctrine but a person, and that person has destroyed death itself. St. Paul, writing to the Corinthians, calls the risen Christ the first fruits of those who have died, the beginning of a harvest that will consume death itself, the last enemy destroyed in the risen body of Jesus Christ, raised on the third day just as he said. The same person who told them three times what would happen, who met them on the road before they had even delivered the angel's message, who still comes to us before we are ready and have everything figured out.

The risen Christ, the one who came back to two frightened women and said, "Rejoice." That is the word Matthew uses: "kairate." It is the ordinary Greek word for "hello," and we heard it as "greetings." But at its root, it means joy. And joy, in this case, is not a feeling; it is a person we encounter. The risen Christ, who met two terrified women on the road and said "Rejoice," is the same one who meets us here at this altar under the forms of bread and wine. He is not in that tomb. He is risen as he said, and he keeps coming to frightened women on a road, to doubting disciples behind locked doors, to you and to me this morning at this altar with our fears and our joys.

This morning, I invite you, as the angel invited the women: "Come and see the place where he lay," and let the empty tomb send you, as it sent them, running toward the one who is already on his way to meet you. Amen.
