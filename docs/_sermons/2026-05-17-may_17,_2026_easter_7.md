A question that everyone here has probably asked at some point in our lives. It's the question that every parent dreads, often asked on repeat by overexcited, overstimulated, or overtired children who are bored with sitting still for hours on a trip. The Israelites ask the same question as they wander for years in the wilderness. They ask the same question during the siege of Jerusalem as God's people look for an end to violence and oppression from empire. And in today's reading from Acts, the Apostles essentially ask the same question while sitting with Jesus, awaiting his next move: Are we there yet? Is now the time?

It's hard to be patient when we wait for something we've anticipated for a really long time. When we're excited to reach our destination, whether it's a promised trip to Disney World or looking toward the promised land, filled with joy and wonder at what comes next, being told "not yet" or "we still have a long way to go" can be excruciating. When will we get there? We're tired of being in the car; this trip is taking forever. Even the people closest to Jesus struggled with the waiting. Even after witnessing God's incredible power for healing and provision, even after the promise of resurrection comes true, and even before Jesus ascends and sends the Holy Spirit, the Apostles want to jump to the final destination. They want the end of the story, the happily ever after, the restoration that they think will solve all their problems.

They want the tangible final fulfillment of Scripture's prophecy that God's people will be restored to their rightful place, enjoying the power, privilege, and prestige as citizens of God's kingdom that conquers and destroys their oppressors: Rome, Babylon, Assyria. They want the reversal, the overturning of what their people have suffered for millennia. "Is this the time when you will restore the kingdom of God to Israel?" Jesus' answer hardly satisfies that long-held yearning: "It is not for you to know. The Holy Spirit will give you power to be my witnesses." And then Jesus leaves in a miraculous blaze of glory. He leaves his followers in the waiting, in the uncomfortable in-between of what has been and what yet will be. Without a roadmap, without the person to encourage and teach the way, they have only two things to hold on to: Jesus' vague promise that the Holy Spirit will come and that they have a job to do.

Their task is to be a witness to Jesus' resurrection and God's promise of eternal life. They are to witness not just in the familiar surroundings of Jerusalem, but to step way beyond their comfort zone into the unknown so they can share God's good news with all the far ends of the earth. Not restoration, but wilderness and wandering and unknowns once again. That hardly seems like good news. But isn't this true of all the mountaintop moments in our lives? Eventually, we leave our play at Disney World. The wedding day of our dreams fades into the everyday mundanity of marriage. The silence and peace of retreat give way to the usual anxieties and people around us who make it difficult to live and love as a good person ought.

Inevitably, we come down from the mountain and return to a messy, exasperating life full of everyday problems and problem people, conflict and chaos and imperfection and exhaustion. Our spouse leaves their clothes lying everywhere when we've just cleaned. The minister at church doesn't practice what they preach. Someone hurts our feelings and doesn't apologize. Even when things are going relatively well in our lives, the journey is long and demanding, and we get tired of being in the car. In those moments when empire looms large and heavy, threatening to uproot the seeds we've sown to anchor our lives, it hardly seems fair that God asks us to wait a little longer and tells us we have more work to do.

Perhaps this is why, for many Christians, worship and religion focus so heavily on the heavenly afterlife. Who doesn't want to dream about angel choirs and harps, golden mansions with many rooms, lazing about on fluffy white clouds? It's the religious version of Disney World, our ultimate happily ever after. It's so much easier to stand looking up toward heaven, waiting for God to come back to rescue us. Everyone wants to be empowered by the Holy Spirit. It's risky to open our lives to the chaotic creativity of the one who blows where she wills. There's no telling where we might be called or what might happen to us or who we might be asked to show forgiveness and love.

We will be given tasks to bring about God's vision for wholeness. We will be accountable for things done and left undone, for what we help heal and for what we harm. We will certainly face conflict, wrestling with our own egos or even with the strongholds of empire in the world. If there's anything that the Easter story shows us, it's that suffering and resurrection are inextricably entwined. So many Christians would rather the Spirit just stay away. Such discipleship is too high a calling at too high a cost. But for those who truly seek to know God, such discipleship, such empowerment is not a burden but a gift. A gift that makes our journey far fuller, richer, and more meaningful than a life lived without the Spirit.

If we are afraid of change, afraid of what our neighbors might think of us, afraid to risk for the sake of the gospel, we will never enjoy the journey. We will be bored and restless, always seeking the mountaintop high, the feel-good religion, the destination. In focusing on escape or eternity, we will miss the actual experience of God in our midst here and now. And so the angels remind us too: What are you waiting for? God's presence, provision, and protection are already accomplished in Christ Jesus, but the fullness of God's story is not yet complete. The time and the ending are not for you to know, but you do get to help write the story in concert with the one who invites us to come together to eat at the banquet table of abiding love and to play with wonder and joy with the Spirit, whose power working in us can do infinitely more than we can ask or imagine. 

We rest in this promise, and in this, we find good news as we pray: Come, Holy Spirit, come.
