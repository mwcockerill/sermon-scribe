In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. This passage from the Gospel of Matthew is a great passage for Christian militants, or so it seems at first. "Go out into the light, shout from the rooftops. Christ came not to bring peace, but a sword. Cling not to your fathers and mothers, but to Jesus. Cling not to this life, but give up your life for the sake of Christ." There is no middle-of-the-road, conciliatory Christianity here. No compromise, no accommodation, no middle path. Beyond a call to be a Christian militant, the reading goes, Jesus invites his disciples to share in his fate. This requires not only the loss of family and being outcast socially, but active hostility to the point of death.

In this way of reading it, Jesus encourages his disciples to live beyond fear. They know that God cares for them more than they can possibly care for anything themselves. They likewise know that their confession of Jesus wins them his recognition on the last day. These words have brought peace to many Christians over the centuries, but they can also bring fear and trepidation. This passage is a useful passage for those militant Christians who somehow enjoy confronting both fear and power. I don't know about you, but I wouldn't describe myself as a Christian militant. I no longer feel comfortable singing that old hymn, "Stand Up, Stand Up for Jesus, Ye Soldiers of the Cross." I left that construct when I chose to leave my Southern Baptist roots. This way of reading Matthew 10 makes me want to go take a shower.

But providentially, a deeper dive reveals a different message entirely. In the beginning of the passage, Jesus says to his disciples, "A disciple is not above the teacher, nor a slave above the master. It is enough for the disciple to be like the teacher and the slave like the master." The word "disciple" in Greek simply means "learner." So to be a disciple of Jesus is to be a learner from Jesus. Discipleship means to ask this question: What is Jesus teaching, seeking to teach us, and what are we to learn from it? Perhaps the point of this passage is to pause and learn from Jesus who we are to be, what we are to say, and how we are to interact with others. Perhaps it's about what it means to be obedient to the Lord.

We tend to think of obedience in terms of power, and we think of power as control or domination. Someone has power, and someone else submits to that power and is thus obedient. We hear Jesus say, "I have not come to bring peace but a sword, and I have come to set a man against his father." If we read these verses with this understanding of obedience in mind, we end up right where we started, feeling like Jesus is calling us to be Christian militants. But notice what the sword in this passage actually is. It is not a weapon Jesus hands his disciples to take up. The very same saying appears in Luke's Gospel, and there the word is not "sword," but "division." The sword Jesus brings is the rupture that loyalty to him can drive through a household—the cost of discipleship, not its weapon.

The surrounding verses help us to see all of this in a different light. Jesus tells us that we are valued. We are called to have no fear of those who persecute us. Jesus tells us that secrets will become known, that things said in the dark should be told in the light. What is heard whispered should be proclaimed from the housetops. The disciples, and we, are not only called to learn from Jesus, but also to share the love we have received from him with those around us. While we Episcopalians aren't known for being enthusiastic evangelists, in fact, evangelism can be somewhat of a dirty word in our circles. The idea of being a fearless witness is not based on the authoritarian model of obedience in which one person submits to another because they have power over them and are afraid of them.

No, we are told not to fear each other, but to fear God, who gives Jesus the authority to proclaim these seemingly bizarre verses. Discipleship and the evangelism that necessarily comes with it is not something we do out of fear. It's not something we do out of obedience to someone who has power and to whom we are subservient. Discipleship and evangelism are things we do without fear, out of obedience to the one who took up his cross for our sake and gave everything to redeem us. The word "obedience" actually means to listen intently and to respond. To be obedient to Christ is to listen to him and to respond.

This virtue of Christian obedience is given an entire chapter in St. Benedict's monastic rule from the 6th century. In order to listen to the voice of Jesus, St. Benedict calls on his monks to listen to their abbot, to their other superiors, and to the voices of the other monks. You and I, of course, are not Benedictine monks, but the principle still applies. We are called to be obedient to Christ by listening both to the voices of those who have authority over us and to the voices of all those around us. That is to say, obedience is listening to the voice of God wherever it may be found and responding.

This may sound easy, but every time we face a choice or make a decision, we find ourselves in particular circumstances to which we must be obedient—whether it's at work, at home, with friends, or at church. There are obvious relationships where obedience is expected. Priests are expected to be obedient to their bishop. Workers are supposed to be obedient to their boss. Children to their parents, etc. But Christian obedience is to listen to the voice of Christ in all those around us and to respond. I'm called to listen to the voice of Christ in the voice of my spouse, in the voices of my friends, my nieces, and in the voices of all of you.

Sometimes we hear the voice of God and say to ourselves, "Fine, I guess I will change my behavior. It's not really what I want to do, but I'll do it because I'm supposed to." In his rules, St. Benedict doesn't call for reluctant obedience that manifests as mere outward conformity despite our grumblings, but rather an interior obedience that comes cheerfully, freely, and gladly. He says that unhesitating obedience comes naturally to those who cherish Christ above all.

Dear friends, unhesitating obedience to Jesus does indeed come with a cost. That division Jesus named, the sword that is not a weapon but a rupture, can run right through our closest relationships. Jesus said, "I have not come to bring peace but a sword. I have come to set a man against his father and a daughter against her mother." Sometimes obedience to Christ costs us dearly. It may cause division in our relationships, even within our own families. But thanks be to God, Jesus promises us that those who find their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it. 

We hear a similar promise from St. Paul in our second lesson: "If we have been united with him in a death like his, we will certainly be united with him in a resurrection like his." I don't think that Jesus's words in this strange passage are designed to turn us all into militant Christians who love to sing "Stand Up, Stand Up for Jesus." Rather, today's call is to be a disciple of Christ, to learn from him who we are to be, what we are to say, and how we are to interact with others. We are called to unhesitating, joyful obedience to Christ.
