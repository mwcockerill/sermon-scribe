When someone we love dies, especially someone whose life was as full and multifaceted as Joaquin's, there is a natural temptation to try to say everything—to catalog their achievements, to explain why this particular life was so important. But the Christian faith gives us another way. At a requiem mass like this one, the Church does not ask us to summarize Joaquin's life. She asks us to place it in God's hands and to trust that God knows exactly what to do with it.

The prophet Isaiah gives us language for that trust. He speaks of God giving a garland instead of ashes, the oil of gladness instead of mourning, the mantle of praise instead of a faint spirit. Isaiah gives us words rooted in ordinary human experience, in bodies that tire, in lives that are spent, in gestures repeated over time. And he dares to say that God meets all of it with joy.

Joaquin understood that kind of faith. He was not someone who floated about above the material world. He lived out his devotion to God through practice, through showing up, through doing things carefully, reverently, and faithfully over time. Many of us knew Joaquin not so much as a PhD academic—though he certainly was that—but rather as a simple server at the altar. He took that role very seriously. He cared about the details because he believed that God meets us not primarily in the academy, but in the simple creatures of bread and wine made holy by the power of the Holy Spirit and the hands of a priest.

He showed up at mass after mass, multiple times a week, year after year, not because he was seeking recognition, but because service was how he prayed. It was how he loved God, and it was how he loved this community. In a culture that prizes novelty and visibility, Joaquin quietly bore witness to something older and truer. The faithfulness over time forms the soul. That holiness often looks like steady reliability. The work of God is usually carried forward by people who simply show up to serve.

Joaquin's life was not confined to this place, of course. He had a brilliant and far-reaching academic career. He was a scholar, a teacher, a man of intellect and accomplishment. But what strikes so many of us, especially here at Atonement, is that none of that made him aloof or haughty. He did not imagine that credentials exempted him from humility. He wouldn't even let his nurse at Paul House call him Dr. Viejas. He came to the altar with clear-eyed reverence for what the church was doing there.

That is what makes Isaiah's promise ring so true to us today—a garland instead of ashes. Ashes are what remain when something has been burned down, when energy has been spent, when strength has been exhausted. Joaquin's strength was certainly exhausted beginning with his first stroke last summer. He suffered greatly during his final months, and it was painful to witness. But his final months are not the sum of his life. They are but one chapter, not the whole book.

The audacious Christian claim is that God does not define us by our most diminished season. God does not remember us primarily by our losses, our illnesses, our final struggles, or even our greatest accomplishments. At the baptismal font, Joaquin was sealed as Christ's own forever. That promise is stronger than illness and more enduring even than death. We are not finally defined by what we achieve or what we endure, but by whose we are—claimed by God in the waters of baptism and held fast in God's love forever.

The pain Joaquin endured is no more. The confusion, the diminishment, the sorrow—these are not what God is holding now. God holds the entire whole Joaquin: the servant, the scholar, the friend, the man who showed up and paid attention and gave himself to mundane service in the background. The same God whom Joaquin trusted enough to serve week after week is the God who now welcomes him fully. The God whose presence he honored at this altar has not failed him.

The altar he served so faithfully has always pointed beyond itself, to a dwelling place where the God we have known in the Eucharist is before us face to face. In our Gospel reading, when Jesus speaks of preparing a place, he is not talking about fancy architecture or square footage. He is talking about communion—being gathered into God's own life. Joaquin practiced that communion here, imperfectly as we all do, but sincerely and over an entire lifetime. That communion does not end with death, but it does change. What Joaquin practiced here in signs and sacraments he now knows in fullness.

And yet for us who remain, the change is real. We grieve the absence of his presence even as we trust the promise that he is more fully in God's presence now. And so today, friends, we grieve, but we do not grieve as those without hope. Our hope is not based on wishful thinking or the denial of death; it is based on the character of God—a God who delights in faithful service, who honors lives poured out in love, and who keeps his promises.

Isaiah gives us words for what happens when a life has reached its end and is placed entirely in God's hand. When human strength has been spent, when all that remains is what has been lived and offered, God does not discard it. God receives it, gathers it up, and transforms it according to a mercy beyond our comprehension. Joaquin served at the altar with care and devotion, and now, by God's mercy, he is received at that altar's fulfillment in heaven, where nothing good is wasted, nothing faithful is forgotten, and nothing broken is left unhealed.

May our friend Joaquin rest in peace and rise in glory, and may we who remain on this side of the veil be strengthened by his example to keep showing up, to keep serving well, and to trust that God is at work in every faithful act. Until that day, when we are all gathered at that heavenly banquet with Joaquin and those.
